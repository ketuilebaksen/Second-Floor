The most important decision the Pittsburgh Steelers made this week wasn't a play call. It wasn't a lineup change. It wasn't even the trade itself. It was a choice that comes from the very top of this organization, from the ownership level, from the way the Rooney family has run this franchise for decades. And after a 27-24 loss in Cleveland, with Joey Porter Jr. now wearing a star on his helmet and saying he felt "disrespected and undervalued," that choice is about to be questioned louder than it has been in years.

Here's the thing nobody is saying out loud. Art Rooney II hasn't stood at a podium this week. He hasn't released a statement about Porter. But make no mistake: when a franchise refuses to negotiate contracts during the season, and that refusal ends with one of its best young players shipped out for a pick two drafts away, that is an ownership decision. That's the Steelers way. And today we're asking whether the Steelers way still works.

And understand how rare change is in this building. Since 1969, the Steelers have had four head coaches. Chuck Noll. Bill Cowher. Mike Tomlin. And now Mike McCarthy. That's it. Four in more than half a century. That kind of stability doesn't happen by accident. It comes from an ownership philosophy that values patience, loyalty, and doing things one specific way. It built six Lombardi Trophies. It's why so many fans trust this family. But that same philosophy also means that when something is broken, the people at the top are very slow to change it.

Let's start with the policy, because a lot of younger fans don't know how deep this goes. The Steelers have a long-standing rule: once the regular season starts, contract talks stop. No in-season extensions. Period. Omar Khan said it plainly on August 31st. The team does not deal with extensions during the season. It's not a Khan rule. It's not a McCarthy rule. It has been part of how this organization operates for a long time, under Rooney leadership, through multiple general managers and head coaches.

And to be fair, it has worked a lot of the time. The pressure of a hard deadline forces both sides to get serious. Troy Polamalu got his deal done right before the season back in 2011. Cam Heyward got his new deal done before last season. Pittsburgh writer Nick Farabaugh put it well when he said the Steelers haven't had a ton of turmoil in-season, because the deadline spurs action and they usually get the business done. That's the case for the policy. Clean, disciplined, no circus during the season.

But here's the other side. When it doesn't work, it doesn't just fail. It explodes. Le'Veon Bell is the name every Steelers fan remembers. A standoff that never got solved, a season lost, and a player who walked. And now Joey Porter Jr. joins that list. The policy set the clock. The clock ran out on Week 1. And from that moment, the only two outcomes were "play on your rookie deal" or "leave." Porter chose neither. So the organization chose for him.

Let me rebuild the timeline quickly, because the order of events is the whole story. In the offseason, according to Ari Meirov, Pittsburgh was quietly open to moving Porter, but wanted a 2027 first-round pick. Nobody bit. The extension talks went nowhere. According to CBS Sports, the Steelers offered around 27 million a year, and according to James Palmer, Porter was looking for at least 30. The market jumped when Christian Gonzalez and Devon Witherspoon signed massive deals. Porter spent the summer on the PUP list with a back injury and missed the first three games. Then, the week of the Browns game, he practiced fully on Monday and Tuesday, his back flared up on Wednesday, he was ruled out, and that same night he was traded to Dallas for a 2028 second-round pick and a conditional 2027 sixth.

From a 2027 first in the spring to a 2028 second in the fall. That's what happens when a policy turns into a stalemate and the stalemate turns into a fire sale.

And this isn't a one-time thing. Look at the pattern. George Pickens, traded to Dallas. Broderick Jones, traded to Dallas this summer. Now Joey Porter Jr., traded to Dallas. Broderick Jones and Porter were the first two picks of Omar Khan's very first draft as general manager in 2023. Both are gone, and both to the same team. When you draft and develop your own players, which is supposed to be the whole identity of this franchise, you can't keep turning them back into picks right when they become worth paying. At some point, the people at the top have to look at that pattern and ask whether the system is producing the outcome they actually want.

Now, let me be crystal clear about one thing. I'm not accusing anybody of anything regarding that back. I don't know what happened medically, and neither do you. Porter says his back is feeling better and the Cowboys are hoping he can debut Sunday against Houston. That could be completely legitimate. Backs are strange. What I care about is the organizational decision, not a guy's MRI.

And then Porter said the words that are going to echo around Pittsburgh all season. He "definitely felt disrespected and undervalued." When a player who grew up around this franchise, whose father is a Steelers legend, uses the word "disrespected," that should hit the front office and the owner's box. Because the Steelers sell themselves as family. As the organization that treats its own people right. That's the brand. And the son of one of their legends just walked out the door saying the opposite.

Jalen Ramsey, who's been traded himself, stood up for Porter afterward. He said "It's not all on Joey," and that the Steelers chose what route they wanted to go as well. That's important. When a respected veteran in your own locker room publicly says the organization shares the responsibility, you have to listen.

And Mike McCarthy? He backed the organization all the way. He called it the modern NFL and said, "As an organization, we felt like this was the best thing for us, and we wish Joey well." Notice the words. "As an organization." That's not a coach making a football decision. That's a coach speaking for the building. For the front office. And ultimately, for ownership.

And think about the message it sends to the locker room. You trade a homegrown starter on a Wednesday night, hours before a short-week division road game. Players notice that. Young players especially notice that. The guys who are a year or two away from their own second contracts are watching how this ends. If the lesson they take away is "do everything right, get to your payday, and then get shipped out," that's a culture problem, and culture is supposed to be the Steelers' biggest advantage. The best organizations make their young stars believe the second contract is coming. Ownership has to make sure that belief is still alive in Pittsburgh.

So now let's put this decision next to what actually happened on the field Thursday night, because that's where the pressure on ownership really starts.

Pittsburgh lost 27-24 in Cleveland and dropped to 2-2. The Browns are 3-1 and alone in first place in the AFC North. The game turned in the second quarter. Pittsburgh led 7-0, then Denzel Ward intercepted Aaron Rodgers, and Cleveland went on a 21-0 run before halftime, gashing this defense for 191 yards and three touchdowns in that quarter alone. And here's the part that ties directly back to the Porter decision. Jamel Dean, the veteran corner signed this offseason, the guy who was supposed to be the outside answer after Porter left, hurt his ankle in that same second quarter and didn't come back. He was in a boot after the game.

So in the first game after the trade, the secondary ended up with Jalen Ramsey playing with a broken wrist, Brandin Echols just cleared from a concussion, Asante Samuel Jr. coming off a benching, and rookie Daylen Everette thrown into heavy snaps on the road in prime time. Cleveland's rookie receivers, Denzel Boston and KC Concepcion, combined for 153 yards. Cameron Heyward said it after the game. Not close enough, not enough contested throws.

Now, to be fair, Porter wasn't going to play in this game anyway. He was already ruled out. So the trade didn't cost them Thursday night directly. But it absolutely shapes the rest of the season. Because every time this secondary struggles from now until the deadline, fans are going to point at the owner's box and ask why one of the best young cover corners in the AFC is in Dallas.

And the team didn't just lose in the secondary. Derrick Harmon was carted off with a foot injury in the first half. The Steelers committed 14 penalties for 88 yards. Rodgers was sacked five times. And there was that sideline exchange between Rodgers and McCarthy that Prime Video caught, with frustration boiling over about plays coming in too slowly. McCarthy owned his part afterward, saying he wished he hadn't used those words. That was the right thing to do. But it also tells you the operation still isn't clean in Week 4.

And yet this team still fought. Rodgers led two fourth-quarter touchdown drives, ran in the two-point conversion himself, and tied it with 1:42 left. T.J. Watt had a monster night with a sack, a forced fumble and a fumble recovery. DK Metcalf had 115 yards. Robert Spears-Jennings picked off his first career pass. Then Deshaun Watson went 35 yards in seven plays and Andre Szmyt hit a 56-yarder with ten seconds left. A team with fight, losing on the margins.

Okay. Now let's talk about ownership's own words, because this is where it gets really interesting.

Back in July, Art Rooney II laid out two priorities for 2026. Number one, he said the offense was statistically good last year and hopefully could be better. Number two, he said this team needs to stop the run better than it has recently. Those were his words. That was the owner's checklist.

So how's that going? On offense, honestly, it's been up and down but trending up. The Bengals game was the best performance of the year, and the Browns game had 299 passing yards and a fourth-quarter comeback. I'll give that one a cautious thumbs up. But stopping the run? Cleveland scored two rushing touchdowns on Thursday, including a 28-yard run by Jaleel McLaughlin. The defense has been leaky in key moments. The owner's own priority isn't being met yet.

And now go back a couple of years. Rooney said publicly that the organization had had enough of the playoff drought. Think about where that drought stands. The Steelers haven't won a playoff game since January 2017. Last season they won the AFC North, hosted a playoff game, and got beat badly by Houston in the Wild Card round. Mike Tomlin, after 19 seasons, is gone. Mike McCarthy is in. Aaron Rodgers, 42 years old, is the quarterback.

Steelers Nation, you don't hire a veteran head coach and keep a 42-year-old quarterback because you're thinking about 2028. That is a win-now decision. Ownership signed off on it. So here's the contradiction that's going to bother a lot of you. The same organization that went all-in on win-now at head coach and quarterback just traded a starting cornerback in his prime for a pick that won't even be used until 2028. Those two decisions don't fit in the same sentence.

Now, let me give you ownership's side, because I promised you balance. From their view, Porter wanted top-of-the-market money, had a recurring back problem, and hadn't played a snap all season. Paying 30-plus million a year to a corner with that injury history is a real risk. And the league seemed to agree, because multiple teams reportedly thought Pittsburgh's asking price plus Porter's contract demands were too expensive. Ownership has always prided itself on not making panic decisions with its money. You could argue this is them sticking to that discipline. And there's a longer-term view too. That money can now help keep young offensive linemen like Zach Frazier, Mason McCormick and Troy Fautanu together when their deals come up. That's a defensible plan.

But here's my take, and I want to be clear this is opinion, not a report. Discipline only matters if it produces wins. The Steelers' approach has given this city a lot of stability, a lot of winning seasons, and two Super Bowls under the current ownership era. Nobody's taking that away. But stability without playoff wins isn't the goal. The goal is the seventh ring. And when the market moves faster than your policy, the policy has to evolve, or you keep losing players at exactly the moment they become worth paying.

So what should ownership actually do? Here's my three-point plan for the people at the top.

Number one: keep the deadline, but start earlier. If you're not going to negotiate in-season, then the serious talks with your young core need to start a year earlier, not in August. Get ahead of the market instead of chasing it. Look at who's next. Your young offensive linemen, your rising defensive pieces. Don't let any of them reach the August cliff without a real plan.

Number two: give Omar Khan the green light to be aggressive before the trade deadline. This secondary is hanging by a thread. Dean's ankle, Ramsey's wrist, a rookie learning on the fly. If the owner's box truly believes in this win-now window, then use some of those extra picks, maybe even that 2028 second, on a real cornerback. Not another aging stopgap. We've done the Patrick Peterson, Levi Wallace, Darius Slay rotation. Go get somebody who can stay.

And while we're at it, take a hard look at the policy itself. Plenty of teams around the league are willing to finalize extensions after the season kicks off when the right deal is on the table. I'm not saying the Steelers have to become a franchise that negotiates in a panic every October. I'm saying there's a middle ground. A rule that exists to avoid distractions shouldn't force you to sell a starting cornerback at a discount in the middle of a playoff push. When the rule creates a bigger distraction than the one it was designed to prevent, the rule needs an exception.

Number three: say something. Steelers fans are some of the most loyal in all of sports. They fill that stadium no matter what. When a player says he felt disrespected and the team drops to 2-2 the next day, the fan base deserves to hear from the top. Not a press release. A real answer about the vision. Fans can handle a tough decision. What they hate is silence.

Now let's look ahead, because the schedule just gave this team a gift. Since they played on Thursday, the Steelers don't play again until Sunday, October 11th. Ten full days. That's time to heal Ramsey's wrist, get clarity on Dean's ankle and Harmon's foot, and fix the operational issues that had a 42-year-old quarterback screaming for the play call. It's also time for the front office to make a move if they're going to make one.

Every Steelers fan is going to be watching that game, and every one of them is going to be thinking about the owner's box.

So Steelers Nation, here's where I land. Ownership didn't make one shocking announcement this week. What they did was let a long-standing principle make the decision for them. And if the policy keeps getting in the way of that standard, then it's the policy that has to change, not the standard.
