Steelers Nation, Mike McCarthy has made a decision about this offense, and if you’d told me a month ago that he’d make it, I wouldn’t have believed you. The head coach who came into this season leaning on one of the most pass-heavy attacks in the NFL is now building his offense around a lineman lining up as an extra blocker. On Monday, McCarthy made it clear that Spencer Anderson’s role isn’t a one-week gimmick. At the same time, he was brutally honest about the one thing that’s still holding this team back, and his star receiver has already offered a solution. And with receivers banged up, some fans are now dreaming about a big-name trade target out of Detroit. So today, we’re breaking down McCarthy’s decision, his honest admission about the red zone, the Jameson Williams idea, and what all of it means for Sunday’s showdown with the Browns. This is James with Second Floor. Let’s go.

The decision: Spencer Anderson’s new role.

Let’s start with the decision itself. In Paris, the Steelers rolled out what’s being called an ultra-jumbo package for the first time this season. Offensive lineman Spencer Anderson lined up as an extra blocker, reporting as eligible, and the Steelers used it to pound the Saints on the ground. Jaylen Warren ran for 29 yards on the opening drive, and the Steelers finished with 148 rushing yards.

On Monday, McCarthy explained why Anderson matters so much. “When you have someone like Spencer who has unique talents, it gives you such great versatility,” he said. And he added, “He carries a lot of value on our football team, especially up front.”

That tells me this package is here to stay. According to Steeler Nation, the expectation is that Anderson’s sub-package will continue, partly because of the injuries at receiver. When you don’t have a full room of healthy receivers, putting an extra big body on the field and running the ball makes a lot of sense.

Anderson’s comeback story.

And there’s a great story behind Anderson. He opened the season as the starting right guard, but he struggled in the first two weeks. It turned out he was playing through a broken bone in his leg, which likely affected his performance. While he recovered, Brock Hoffman stepped in at right guard and played well.

Anderson returned in Week 7, and instead of trying to force him back into his old job, McCarthy found him a new one. Swing tackle. Extra blocker. The sixth lineman in a package that just helped the Steelers run over a team. That’s good coaching: when a player has unique skills, you find a way to use them instead of leaving him on the bench.

The offensive line is coming together.

Anderson’s new role is part of a bigger story up front. Brock Hoffman, who stepped in at right guard, is a veteran who knows McCarthy’s system from their time together in Dallas. First-round rookie Max Iheanachor has taken over at right tackle and keeps getting better. And now Anderson gives the line a sixth body who can move around and play multiple spots.

That kind of depth is exactly what you need to run the ball 30 times a game. Running the ball isn’t just about the backs. It’s about five, or in this case six, linemen executing their blocks together. Putting another lineman on the field doesn’t automatically fix anything if people miss assignments. What encouraged me in Paris was seeing the backs consistently get through the line and get to the second level. That’s a sign the whole unit is on the same page.

And it helps Rodgers too. Every productive run means a more manageable second or third down. A 42-year-old quarterback doesn’t need to be standing in the pocket on third-and-long against a pass rush all afternoon. When the line is controlling the line of scrimmage, Rodgers gets easier throws and more time.

Why this decision is so surprising.

To understand why this is such a big shift, look at where the Steelers were two weeks ago. Coming into Paris, according to NFL.com, Pittsburgh had the second-highest pass rate in the NFL. In Tampa, according to one widely shared column, Rodgers dropped back 54 times and was sacked five times, while the Steelers ran only 21 times. Critics were calling the play-calling unsustainable. One column argued McCarthy was nearly costing the team games by abandoning the run.

McCarthy heard it. And in one week, he went from one of the most pass-happy offenses in football to a team that ran it 30 times and closed the game with an 11-play drive made up entirely of runs. As he put it after the game: “We felt as a football team that we needed to own the line of scrimmage on both sides.”

That’s what makes this decision notable. A coach with a Super Bowl ring and a long track record could have stuck with what he knew. Instead, he adjusted to his personnel. With Michael Pittman Jr. and Roman Wilson on injured reserve and Ben Skowronek nursing a calf injury, he built the offense around what he has: two strong running backs, a big tight end, and a versatile lineman.

Two backs, one plan.

The decision to lean on the run also settles last week’s backfield debate. In Tampa, people criticized how McCarthy split the snaps between Warren and Dowdle. In Paris, both got real work. Warren carried 18 times for 101 yards. Dowdle carried nine times for 41 yards and contributed in the passing game, despite being questionable all week with a knee injury.

McCarthy explained the thinking on Monday: “When we signed Rico, we knew we had two really big time backs we could lean on.” Rodgers summed up Warren’s consistency this way: “We’re not going to get numb to it, but we’re almost expecting Jaylen to have 100 yards.” When you have two backs like that, a jumbo package isn’t just a gimmick. It’s a way to get your best players the ball in the situations where they’re most dangerous.

McCarthy’s honest admission: the red zone.

But McCarthy didn’t sugarcoat the problem. After the game, he was asked about the red zone, and he was blunt. “Yeah, one in four is not where you want to be clearly,” he said. “No doubt about it.” He also explained the approach: “We wanted to, you know, take control of the clock. But yeah, we definitely want to score touchdowns down there.”

Now, to be fair, it was really one for three. The fourth trip ended with Rodgers kneeling down to run out the clock. But the point stands. According to Steeler Nation, the Steelers came into this game ranked 19th in the NFL in red zone touchdown rate, at 53 percent. Chris Boswell went 3-for-3 on field goals and saved the day, but a team that wants to contend can’t keep settling for three points inside the 20.

And McCarthy’s attitude is the right one: “It’s always good to win and have things to work on.”

DK Metcalf’s red zone solution.

Here’s where it gets interesting. DK Metcalf has already offered an answer. According to Athlon Sports, Metcalf said that whenever the Steelers get into the red zone, Darnell Washington “should definitely get an opportunity to catch a touchdown.”

And he has a point. Washington caught the Steelers’ only offensive touchdown in Paris, a 4-yard fade from Rodgers, and it was his second touchdown of the season. At his size, he’s a nightmare matchup near the goal line, and he’s already on the field in those heavy packages as a blocker. That’s the beauty of it: the same formation that sets up the run can also produce a touchdown pass to a 6-foot-7 target if the defense crowds the line.

Pat Freiermuth is part of this too. All of his touchdowns this season have come in the red zone. Between Washington and Freiermuth, the Steelers have two tight ends who can win near the goal line. The question is whether McCarthy will call those plays when it matters, instead of settling for a field goal.

Why the red zone matters so much against Cleveland.

And this matters right now. The Browns beat the Steelers 27-24 in Week 4, a game decided by three points. When you play good teams, you can’t leave touchdowns on the field. Against the Saints, three field goals were enough. Against Cleveland, at home, with first place in the AFC North on the line, the Steelers will probably need touchdowns.

On the other hand, the Steelers’ defense has been excellent in its own red zone. According to Steeler Nation, Pittsburgh has allowed touchdowns on only 50 percent of opponent trips inside the 20, which ranks seventh in the league. Payton Wilson’s interception in Paris came with the Saints threatening inside the red zone. If the defense keeps holding opponents to field goals, and the offense starts finishing drives, that’s a winning combination.

The play-action payoff.

Here’s the next step I want to see from McCarthy’s plan. When a defense sees six linemen and a big tight end on the field, every instinct tells it to load the box and stop the run. That’s exactly when play action becomes deadly. Fake the handoff to Warren, let the linebackers bite, and suddenly Freiermuth is running free up the seam or Washington is slipping into the flat with nobody around him.

We got a glimpse of that in Paris. The 51-yard catch by Metcalf on third-and-14 came in a game where the Saints had spent the afternoon trying to stop the run. When a defense commits extra players near the line, one-on-one matchups open up on the outside, and Metcalf is one of the best in the league at winning those.

That’s how this offense can become more than a run-first unit. The run sets up the pass, the pass keeps the defense honest, and the jumbo package becomes a weapon instead of a tell. If McCarthy mixes those calls well against Cleveland, the Browns’ defense is going to have a very long afternoon.

What the Browns will try to do.

And make no mistake, the Browns will have a plan. They’ll have studied the Paris film. They’ll know Anderson is reporting as eligible, and they’ll probably match the heavy package with more big bodies of their own. This is a team that has found ways to win close games, including coming back from 14-0 down to win in Tennessee last week.

That’s why this game will be a chess match. If Cleveland stacks the box, the Steelers have to throw. If Cleveland plays light to protect against the pass, the Steelers have to run it down their throats. McCarthy’s ability to read that and adjust during the game might decide who leaves Acrisure Stadium in first place.

The Jameson Williams idea.

Now, with the receiver room thin, some analysts have floated a big-name idea: Detroit’s Jameson Williams. And I want to be clear right away. There’s no report that the Steelers are interested in him, and no report that the Lions want to trade him. This is purely a hypothetical.

On the field, it’s easy to see the appeal. Williams has been one of the most explosive deep threats in the NFL. In 2024, he caught 58 passes for 1,001 yards and seven touchdowns, averaging 17.3 yards per catch. In 2025, he caught 65 passes for 1,117 yards and seven more touchdowns, averaging 17.2 yards per catch. A receiver who can take the top off a defense would give Rodgers a weapon that makes the run game and the short passes even more dangerous.

Why Williams isn’t realistic.

But here’s the reality check. In September of 2025, Williams signed a three-year extension with Detroit worth up to $83 million. That’s a major commitment, and the Lions made it because they see him as a core piece of their offense. Teams don’t usually trade players they just extended, especially not in the middle of a season when they’re competing.

And for the Steelers, the cost would be enormous. You’d likely be talking about premium draft picks plus a big contract running several years into the future. That’s not how Omar Khan has built this team. He’s made smart, targeted moves, like claiming cornerbacks off practice squads and bringing back Marquez Valdes-Scantling. A blockbuster for a receiver on a huge deal would be a completely different approach.

So my take is simple. Williams would be an intriguing fit on the field, but the evidence doesn’t support treating a Steelers move as realistic. Unless something changes dramatically in Detroit, this one stays in the dream category. And even if Detroit ever did listen, the price would likely be far beyond what a smart contender should pay in the middle of a season.

The case for continuity.

And there’s a real argument for the Steelers not making a big receiver move at all. They’re 5-2 and winning with a complementary approach. Warren had 101 rushing yards in Paris. Rodgers threw a touchdown. The defense held the Saints to 18 rushing yards. More bodies at receiver don’t automatically mean more production, especially when the offense is now built around the run and the tight ends.

Germie Bernard has caught 17 passes in his first two starts. Metcalf just made a 51-yard catch on third-and-14 that set up the touchdown in Paris. Freiermuth and Washington give the offense two different looks at tight end. And Pittman and Wilson could return from injured reserve later in the season. If the offense keeps producing in close games, continuity has real value.

Trade block: what I’d actually do.

Let’s talk about it, and remember, this is opinion, not a report.

I still think one targeted receiver addition makes sense, but not at the price of a Jameson Williams deal. Josh Downs in Indianapolis is still the more realistic option. The Colts fired their general manager, rival teams expect them to sell, and Downs is a 25-year-old route runner on an expiring contract. He wouldn’t require a massive long-term commitment, and he’d give Rodgers a reliable option on the quick, short throws that keep drives alive.

Mark Kaboly has reported “strong indications” the Steelers will be active before the November 10th deadline. But with McCarthy now leaning on the run and the jumbo package, the bar for a receiver trade just got a little higher. Any addition has to fit what this offense is becoming, not what it was a month ago.

Elliott’s plan and the injury outlook.

McCarthy also shared his plan for DeShon Elliott on Monday. The veteran safety made his season debut in Paris in a limited role, playing fewer than 20 snaps. McCarthy said the Steelers deliberately limited his workload to ease him back in, and that Elliott “cleared that first hurdle.” The plan is to keep building his snaps as the season goes on.

That’s another smart, patient decision, and it fits the pattern of how McCarthy has handled this roster all season: protect the long term while still competing right now. Elliott is a key piece of this defense for the long haul, and there’s no reason to rush him into a full workload in his first week back.

The Browns: the next test of McCarthy’s plan.

So now everything points to Sunday. The Browns come to Acrisure Stadium at 1 p.m., both teams at 5-2, with first place on the line. McCarthy has been clear about what this game means: “We need to beat them. They got us once already, and it’s a rivalry game for us.”

The question is whether his new approach holds up against a better defense. The Browns will have watched the film from Paris. They’ll know about the jumbo package. They’ll probably load the box to stop Warren and Dowdle. That’s when McCarthy’s next decision comes into play: stick with the run, or use the run to set up play action and shots to Metcalf and the tight ends. There’s a big difference between being committed to the run and being predictable.

What I want to see.

Here’s what I want to see on Sunday. Keep the physical identity. Use Anderson and the jumbo package. Feed Warren and Dowdle early. But when the Browns crowd the line, punish them with play action, and when the Steelers get inside the 20, give Washington and Freiermuth chances to win one-on-one. Turn those field goals into touchdowns, and this team becomes very hard to beat, in Pittsburgh or anywhere else.

The bottom line.

So what’s McCarthy’s shocking decision? It’s the turn from a pass-first offense to a physical, run-first attack built around an extra lineman. It’s the decision to keep Spencer Anderson’s jumbo role, because, in McCarthy’s words, “he carries a lot of value on our football team.” And it’s an honest admission that the red zone has to get better, with McCarthy saying “one in four is not where you want to be clearly.”

As for Jameson Williams, he’d be a fun fit, but his contract and the lack of any reported interest make him a long shot. The more realistic path is a smaller, targeted move, and continued growth from the players already here.
