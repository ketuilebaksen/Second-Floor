Steelers Nation, let’s clear the air on the quarterback headline before we do anything else. Aaron Rodgers is still the starter. Nobody has been benched. Nobody has been signed. Nobody has been traded for. Mike McCarthy hasn’t named a successor, and Omar Khan hasn’t announced a plan for 2027. If you clicked expecting a press conference about a new quarterback, there wasn’t one this weekend.

But there is a quarterback decision hanging over this franchise, and it’s tied directly to what happens at the trade deadline on Tuesday, November 10, at 4 p.m. Eastern. The question isn’t who starts in Cincinnati on November 15. It’s who starts in September of next year. And the way the Steelers answer that question is already shaping how aggressive they can afford to be right now.

Today we’re going to break down the three options people keep throwing around: the two young quarterbacks already in the building, the expensive veteran with a direct line to McCarthy, and the 2027 draft class Pittsburgh has been studying for years. Then we’ll connect it to the receiver market, including a division-rival name that’s popped up and the latest twist in the Josh Downs saga. And of course, my board at the end, opinion, not a report.

WHY THE QUARTERBACK QUESTION IS ALREADY HERE

Let’s start with the obvious. Aaron Rodgers is forty-two years old. He came to Pittsburgh to win now, and at six and two, first place in the AFC North, he’s doing exactly that. But nobody, including Rodgers himself, has committed to anything beyond this season. Back in May, ESPN’s Jeremy Fowler reported that Rodgers remained uncommitted to returning, and nothing public has changed since.

That leaves the front office in a tricky spot. Every decision at the deadline has a second layer to it. Spend a premium pick on a receiver now, and that’s one fewer chip for whatever the quarterback plan becomes in the spring. Keep the picks, and you may be leaving a little bit of this season’s ceiling on the table.

So when you hear “quarterback decision,” think of it this way. The decision isn’t about Sunday. It’s about whether this team treats its draft capital as ammunition for the present or insurance for the future. And that’s the lens for everything else in this video.

OPTION ONE: TRUST THE YOUNG QUARTERBACKS

The first path is the one Mark Kaboly of The Athletic has been talking about. According to the transcript this video is based on, Kaboly went on 93.7 The Fan’s afternoon show and argued that once the season ends, Pittsburgh should give its young quarterbacks a genuine chance rather than reaching immediately for another veteran. I couldn’t find a published recap of that specific appearance, so take the details as reported. But it’s consistent with what Kaboly has said for months.

Let’s meet the two players.

Drew Allar was the first pick of the third round in the 2026 draft, number seventy-six overall, out of Penn State. Back in May, Kaboly described him as “hand-picked by McCarthy,” and he suggested Allar had the inside track to start in 2027. In August, during training camp, he added this: “I would think Drew Allar has played a little bit better than him right now,” referring to Will Howard.

Will Howard is in his second NFL season. He was a sixth-round pick in 2025, number one hundred eighty-five overall, after winning a national championship at Ohio State. His rookie year was derailed by a hand injury in early August that kept him out of the preseason, and he never took a regular-season snap. In camp this summer, Kaboly’s assessment was blunt: “Will Howard has a long way to go.” Ray Fittipaldo of the Post-Gazette was a little kinder: “Allar is better than I expected him to be. I think he and Howard are close.”

So here’s the case for option one. Two young quarterbacks, both on cheap contracts, both learning behind a future Hall of Famer. An open competition next spring costs you nothing in draft capital and tells you what you actually have. If one of them hits, you’ve solved the most expensive position in sports without paying for it.

And here’s the case against it. Neither player has thrown a meaningful regular-season pass in the NFL. A third-rounder and a sixth-rounder are long shots by definition. If the competition produces a winner who isn’t ready, a contending roster could waste a season.

OPTION TWO: THE EXPENSIVE VETERAN WITH A McCARTHY CONNECTION

The second path is the one that generates headlines: Dak Prescott.

Let me be completely clear about what this is and what it isn’t. There is no report that Dallas wants to trade Prescott. There is no report that Pittsburgh has inquired about him. What exists is a connection, and a lot of speculation built on top of it.

The connection is real. Mike McCarthy coached Prescott in Dallas from 2020 through 2024. He knows how Prescott processes a defense, how he handles a huddle, and how to build a game plan around him. When Prescott signed his extension in 2024, it was a four-year, two hundred forty million dollar deal, done under McCarthy.

The speculation goes like this. In a July column for Sports Illustrated’s Steelers site, the author pointed out that Prescott’s cap hits climb sharply in 2027 and 2028, citing roughly seventy-six million and eighty-six million dollars, and suggested Dallas could eventually look to move on. That’s an opinion piece. It cites no trade talks and no Steelers interest.

So should Steelers fans want this? Let’s think about it honestly. Prescott would be a massive upgrade in stability over a rookie or a second-year quarterback. He’d step into a system his coach already built around him once. But the price would be staggering. You’d be taking on one of the largest contracts in the league, and you’d almost certainly be giving up significant draft capital to Dallas on top of it. For a team that’s been preaching discipline with its picks, that’s a complete change in philosophy.

OPTION THREE: THE 2027 QUARTERBACK CLASS

The third path is the one that tends to get lost in the noise, but it might be the most important.

Back in May, Jeremy Fowler reported that NFL executives believe Pittsburgh could go “big-game hunting” in the 2027 draft. Fowler described it as “a quarterback class it has spent years studying.” That class, as it’s been discussed, includes names like Arch Manning of Texas, Dante Moore of Oregon, Julian Sayin of Ohio State and CJ Carr of Notre Dame.

Here’s the problem for Pittsburgh. If the Steelers keep winning, they’ll be picking late in the first round. The top quarterbacks rarely last that long. Moving up requires picks, and probably a lot of them.

That’s where the deadline connects. Every premium pick the Steelers spend in November is a pick they can’t use to climb the board in April. Kaboly even noted this spring that a first-round rookie quarterback “won’t be ready” right away, which is part of why the Allar conversation exists. But if Pittsburgh falls in love with a prospect in that class, it’ll need its full arsenal.

So when you see the front office being careful with picks right now, this is likely a big part of the reason. They may be protecting the option to go get their quarterback.

COMPARING THE THREE PATHS SIDE BY SIDE

Let’s put the three options next to each other, because seeing them together makes the trade-offs obvious.

The young quarterbacks are the cheapest path in every sense. No draft picks, no big contract, and a full offseason of reps for Allar and Howard. The risk is entirely on the field. If neither is ready, you find out in September, and by then your options are limited.

The veteran path is the most expensive. It solves the quarterback position on day one with a player your head coach already knows, but it costs you money, picks and, possibly, a few years of flexibility. It also doesn’t solve the long-term question. Prescott would be thirty-four next season. You’d be buying a bridge, not a foundation.

The draft path sits in the middle. It costs picks but not salary, and it gives you a chance at a true long-term answer. The catch is that it depends on where you’re picking and how much you’re willing to give to move up. And as Kaboly pointed out, a first-round rookie might not be ready to start right away, which brings Allar back into the picture as a possible bridge.

Notice something? Two of the three paths depend on keeping draft capital intact. That’s why every deadline rumor involving a second- or third-round pick should be read through this lens.

WHAT RODGERS’ SEASON MEANS FOR THE KIDS

There’s one more angle that doesn’t get enough attention. Allar and Howard aren’t just waiting. Every week, they’re sitting in meetings with a four-time MVP and a head coach who spent five seasons coaching Prescott in Dallas. The way Rodgers prepares, the way he reads defenses, the way he manages a huddle, all of that is a free education. Whatever Pittsburgh decides in the spring, the next few months could make that decision easier.

HOW THIS SHAPES THE RECEIVER MARKET

Now let’s bring it back to the deadline, because the receiver hunt is where the quarterback question shows up in real time.

The Steelers need help. Michael Pittman Jr. is on injured reserve with a foot injury, and Roman Wilson’s availability is still something to monitor. DK Metcalf has been inconsistent. Rookie Germie Bernard has been a bright spot, but he can’t do it alone.

So the front office has two kinds of options. Expensive ones that cost premium picks or big contracts. And efficient ones that fill a role without touching the future. Let’s look at one of each.

THE DIVISION RIVAL NAME: JERRY JEUDY

The first is a name that might surprise you: Cleveland Browns receiver Jerry Jeudy.

According to the transcript, Mike DeFabo of The Athletic raised Jeudy as a name worth considering, while acknowledging how unusual a trade between AFC North rivals would be. I wasn’t able to find DeFabo’s original piece, so I’m passing that along as reported.

The transcript also says DeFabo pointed to the relationship between the organizations and the previous Joe Flacco trade. I want to add context here, because that detail could confuse people. The Flacco trade fans remember was the Browns sending him to Cincinnati in October 2025, a move then-Steelers coach Mike Tomlin publicly questioned. So the point isn’t that Pittsburgh and Cleveland have traded with each other. It’s that Cleveland has already shown it’s willing to deal within the division.

Now, the player. Jeudy’s 2024 season was excellent: one thousand two hundred twenty-nine receiving yards and four touchdowns. Then he signed a three-year, fifty-two point five million dollar extension with the Browns. Since then, the production has dropped sharply. The transcript says he has sixteen catches for one hundred seventy-one yards this season. I couldn’t confirm that updated line, but back on October 2, one Steelers-focused writer had him at just five catches for sixty-two yards and wrote that “Jeudy needs a change in the worst way.”

So, is this realistic? Probably not. One fantasy outlet’s headline this week flatly said the Browns are unlikely to trade him and that the price tag is high. And even if Cleveland were open to it, taking on that contract for a receiver in a down year is the opposite of the efficient approach.

THE EFFICIENT OPTION: JOSH DOWNS, AND THE LATEST TWIST

That brings us back to the name that keeps coming up: Josh Downs.

Here’s what’s new this weekend. On November 7, according to a ClutchPoints summary citing Ian Rapoport and Adam Schefter, the Colts are telling teams they already have a fourth-round offer for Downs. The team making that offer wasn’t named. And Indianapolis is still trying to get a third-rounder.

The same report added a note of caution: “It remains to be seen whether the Colts are being truthful about that fourth-round offer being on the table.” That’s a classic deadline tactic. Tell everyone you have a bid, and hope someone panics and goes higher.

Remember the background. Downs requested a trade, according to ESPN’s Fowler. The Colts are two and five, they fired their general manager, and they’ve been selling veterans. Downs is in the final year of his rookie deal. On October 30, reporter Evan Sidery wrote that both the Chiefs and the Steelers had offered Day 3 compensation. And just yesterday, Mark Kaboly called Downs a fit that “seems like a perfect fit in terms of availability and price.”

So who made the fourth-round offer? We don’t know. It could be Kansas City. It could be Pittsburgh. It could be another team entirely. Or, as the report suggests, it could be leverage.

THE CREATIVE PICK IDEA

Here’s where it gets interesting, and it ties directly back to the quarterback question.

The transcript highlights an idea from Christopher Barber at Steeler Nation. I couldn’t locate the original article, so I’ll describe the concept as reported. Pittsburgh doesn’t have its own 2027 fourth-round pick. It went to Dallas in the Broderick Jones trade. So instead, the Steelers could offer a 2028 fourth.

Why does that matter? Because it keeps the 2027 draft capital intact for a potential quarterback move. You’re paying with future money, essentially.

And there’s a second layer. If Downs plays well and then leaves in free agency, Pittsburgh might earn a compensatory pick in return. Comp picks are based on the free agents a team loses and signs, so a player acquired at the deadline can count toward that formula once he hits the market. But the transcript is right to be cautious: there’s no guarantee. It depends on the size of the contract he signs elsewhere, how much he plays, and how active the Steelers are in free agency themselves. So treat it as a possible bonus, not part of the price.

Still, the logic is clean. A 2028 pick for a half-season of a productive slot receiver, with a chance to recoup some value later, all while protecting the 2027 draft. That fits a team trying to win now and plan for its next quarterback at the same time.

WHAT ABOUT MASON RUDOLPH?

One more quarterback note before the board. Earlier this week, Jeremy Fowler named Mason Rudolph as the most realistic trade piece on Pittsburgh’s roster, and Rudolph is signed only through this season.

Here’s why that’s relevant. If the Steelers trust Allar or Howard enough to be the emergency backup behind Rodgers for the rest of the season, Rudolph becomes a tradeable asset. If they don’t, he’s an insurance policy you can’t afford to lose with a forty-two-year-old starter. In a way, how the Steelers handle Rudolph this week might tell you more about their confidence in the young quarterbacks than any quote.

THE BIGGER PICTURE BEFORE CINCINNATI

Let’s zoom out for a second.

Pittsburgh is six and two, first in the AFC North, coming off the bye. The next game is November 15 in Cincinnati against a five-and-two Bengals team. Pittsburgh won the first meeting in Week 3.

The offense has a clear identity in the running game. Jaylen Warren is second in the NFL with nine hundred ninety-eight scrimmage yards. The defense held Cleveland to thirty-four rushing yards and piled up eight sacks in the last game.

What this team needs isn’t a franchise-altering move. It’s a targeted one. A receiver who fits. A pick that doesn’t come out of the 2027 pile. And a front office that keeps both the present and the future in focus.

MY TRADE BLOCK BOARD (OPINION, NOT A REPORT)

Here’s my board. Opinion, not a report.

Josh Downs for a 2028 fourth-round pick: yes. That’s the sweet spot. It protects the 2027 draft and fills the slot. If the Colts truly insist on a third, especially from the 2027 class, I walk away.

Jerry Jeudy: pass. The contract is too big, the production has fallen off, and trading within the division adds a layer of risk I don’t love.

Mason Rudolph: keep him, unless the front office truly believes Allar or Howard is ready to be the backup in a playoff race. I’m not there yet.

Dak Prescott: not a deadline item, and for 2027, I’m out at that price. I’d rather give the young quarterbacks a real look and keep the picks to chase a prospect if one falls into range.

The core: untouchable. Highsmith, Watt, Herbig, Queen, Ramsey, Warren, Frazier.

FINAL WORD

Here’s my bottom line. The Steelers didn’t make a shocking quarterback decision this weekend. But the quarterback question is shaping every decision they make at the deadline. Allar and Howard represent a cheap, uncertain path. Prescott represents an expensive, proven one. The 2027 draft represents the long game. And the way the front office spends its picks over the next few days will tell you which direction it’s leaning.

My hope? A smart, efficient move for Josh Downs that doesn’t touch 2027. Win now, plan for later. That’s the balance.
