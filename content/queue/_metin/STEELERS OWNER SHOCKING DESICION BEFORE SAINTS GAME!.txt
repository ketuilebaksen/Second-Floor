Steelers Nation, there’s a headline going around that says Art Rooney II made a shocking decision before the Saints game. So let me tell you exactly what the Steelers’ owner actually did this week, because the truth is a lot more grounded than the clickbait. Rooney didn’t fire anybody. He didn’t sell anybody. He didn’t overrule anybody. What he did was get on a call with the NFL about safety in Paris, board an overnight flight, and show up in France ready for a historic weekend. Meanwhile, the real decisions this week came from the front office and the coaching staff, and some of them could shape the rest of the season. So today, we’re breaking down Rooney’s role, every roster decision the Steelers made before Paris, the safety rotation question, and the trade options people keep asking about. This is James with Second Floor. Let’s go.

What Rooney actually did this week.

Let’s start with the owner. This week’s Paris trip came with real concerns. France has been dealing with its worst civil unrest in years. The protests began in late September over school conditions and expanded to the cost of living and fuel prices. Some of them turned into rioting, with vehicles burned, businesses damaged, and violent clashes with police. Around 2,400 people were arrested in a single weekend.

So what did the Steelers do? According to KDKA Radio, McCarthy said the team, along with Rooney, joined a Zoom call with the NFL to go over the situation. McCarthy said afterward, “We feel like we have a really good plan, we’re aware of the situations over there.” The league’s executive vice president, Peter O’Reilly, said the NFL is “confident in our security plans” and that officials have been “very vigilant.” He also said the game was never in danger of being moved.

And then Rooney went to Paris himself. The Pittsburgh Post-Gazette reported that he arrived on Thursday after an overnight flight from New York, and that he seemed upbeat. His message about security was that the NFL will have heightened measures in place, but “they don’t seem to be concerned.”

That’s the owner’s decision this week: trust the league’s plan, stand behind the team, and be there in person. Not exactly shocking, but it’s what you want from an owner when there’s uncertainty around a road trip.

Why “shocking” doesn’t fit the Rooney way.

If you know this franchise, you know shocking decisions from the top are rare. From 1969 through 2025, the Steelers had only three head coaches: Chuck Noll, Bill Cowher, and Mike Tomlin. Mike McCarthy is just the fourth since then. That kind of stability doesn’t happen by accident. It comes from an ownership group that hires carefully and then lets people do their jobs.

That’s why the idea of Rooney stepping in with some dramatic midseason move doesn’t really match how this organization operates. When there’s a big decision, like hiring McCarthy or bringing Rodgers back, it goes through a process. And this week, the process was about making sure the team could travel and play safely, not about shaking up the roster.

What the players said about the trip.

The players have been asked about the situation in France too, and their answers were honest. According to KDKA Radio, Jack Sawyer said he was looking forward to the trip, and Payton Wilson said he was excited for the experience. Jaquan Brisker said he’d play, but he admitted he had seen some concerning reports online. That’s a fair reaction. These are young men traveling to a city where there’s been unrest, and it’s natural to have questions.

That’s exactly why the organization’s communication matters. When the owner, the head coach, and the league are all on the same call, walking through the plan, it helps the players focus on football. Keeanu Benton said McCarthy’s message was clear: “it’s a business trip.”

A quick note on Rooney’s week.

There’s one more Rooney story from earlier this month. The Pittsburgh Business Times gave him its Impact Award, its top honor, at a ceremony at Acrisure Stadium. Part of the reason was his role in bringing the 2026 NFL Draft to Pittsburgh, which the article said generated $177 million in regional economic impact. Rooney’s response was simple: “Being successful in business is really about teamwork, surrounding yourself with great people.”

That philosophy shows up in how this organization works. Rooney hired Mike McCarthy, trusted Omar Khan to build the roster, and lets them make the football decisions. And this week, they made a bunch of them.

Decision one: releasing Rayshawn Jenkins.

On Wednesday, the Steelers released veteran safety Rayshawn Jenkins. He’s 32, he started in Week 3, but his snaps had been dropping and he’d been limited to special teams. Pro Football Rumors suggested the move could be about clearing room for DeShon Elliott to come off injured reserve.

Now, some fans are asking who takes Jenkins’ snaps. The honest answer is that he wasn’t playing much defense lately, so this isn’t about replacing a starter. It’s about roster space and trusting the younger players who have earned more time.

Decision two: the safety rotation.

And that brings us to the biggest question in the secondary heading into Sunday. If Elliott comes back, how do the Steelers split the snaps?

Let’s start with Elliott. He practiced fully on Wednesday and Thursday, and his return would bring experience, physicality, and communication to the back end. But he hasn’t played in a regular-season game yet this year, coming off a hyperextended knee and a torn hamstring that required surgery last season. Being healthy enough to practice doesn’t automatically mean he’s ready for 60 snaps. I’d expect the Steelers to build his workload gradually. He was an important part of this defense in 2024 and 2025, a hard hitter and a vocal leader in the back end, and the team needs him for the long haul, not just for one game in Paris. Caution is the smart play.

Then there’s rookie Robert Spears-Jennings. He’s been improving every week, and he’s played well in sub-packages. McCarthy mentioned him by name this week when he said, “I’m proud of our rookie class.” In my opinion, he’s earned the right to keep getting snaps even if Elliott returns.

And then there’s Jaquan Brisker, the toughest call. He took on a bigger role while Elliott was out, and he’s been reliable against the run, finishing with seven tackles in Tampa after briefly leaving with a groin injury. But his consistency has been up and down this season. Patrick Graham has already said there could be competition at the position: “We’ll see what happens this week, but I think the group is really coming together.”

My take? Don’t lock one player into every snap right away. Give Elliott a defined package, keep rewarding Spears-Jennings, and let Brisker respond. Every safety in that room should have a clear role before kickoff, so nobody is guessing on game day. Whatever combination takes the field has to communicate, because the mistakes that hurt this defense early in the season were communication mistakes, not talent mistakes.

Decision three: Joseph-Day to injured reserve.

On Thursday, the Steelers placed defensive lineman Sebastian Joseph-Day on injured reserve with a triceps injury. He’d played in all six games as a reserve, with 10 tackles, a tackle for loss, and a fumble recovery. He’ll miss at least four games, and he joins Derrick Harmon on injured reserve.

That means more snaps for Keeanu Benton, Cam Heyward, and Yahya Black. And honestly, Benton has been earning them. He was strong against the run in Tampa, where the Buccaneers’ lead back, Bucky Irving, finished with just 46 yards on 19 carries. If Benton keeps playing like that, the interior can hold up even with the injuries.

It also leaves the Steelers with two open spots on the 53-man roster, according to Pro Football Talk, after the Jenkins release. One of those spots looks like it’s waiting for Elliott.

Decision four: bringing back Valdes-Scantling.

On offense, the Steelers brought back receiver Marquez Valdes-Scantling on the practice squad. He’s a two-time Super Bowl champion from his time in Kansas City, and he spent four seasons catching passes from Aaron Rodgers in Green Bay, after the Packers drafted him in 2018, McCarthy’s final season as their head coach.

But let’s be realistic about the timing. He arrived in the middle of a week that ends with an overseas flight and a game in a foreign stadium. Even with the familiarity, that’s not an ideal situation for a newcomer. McCarthy’s answer about whether he’d play Sunday was “We’ll see.” My guess is that his bigger contribution comes after Paris, especially with the Browns coming to town, once he’s had a full week of practice.

The wide receiver question.

And that’s the bigger issue for this offense. Michael Pittman Jr. and Roman Wilson are on injured reserve, and Ben Skowronek has been ruled out for Sunday with his calf. DK Metcalf and rookie Germie Bernard are carrying the load. Bernard has caught 17 passes in his first two career starts and scored the game-winning touchdown in Tampa. I’d love to see him put together his first 100-yard game soon. The more he earns Rodgers’ trust, the less Pittsburgh has to depend on a trade.

But fans still want Khan to go get someone, and I understand why.

Trade block: realistic options, not fantasy ones.

Let’s talk about it, and remember, this is opinion, not a report.

The most realistic name to me is still Josh Downs. The Colts fired their general manager on Monday, and ESPN reported that rival teams expect Indianapolis to sell before the deadline. Downs is 25, he’s on an expiring deal, and he’s a route runner who could give Rodgers a reliable option on short and intermediate throws.

Other names have come up, like Jerry Jeudy in Cleveland, Brian Thomas Jr. in Jacksonville, and Rashod Bateman in Baltimore. But I don’t know that any of those teams want to move those players, and two of them play in the AFC North. Trades within the division are rare. And I’m not going to put a draft pick on the table like I know what a deal would cost. Before getting excited about any name, you need to know whether a realistic deal even exists.

Some people have also floated Chris Godwin in Tampa. I understand the appeal of a veteran with his track record. But his contract would have to be part of any conversation, and with Metcalf and Bernard already producing, I’d want a really convincing reason to spend big on another veteran. If Pittsburgh gives up a pick and takes on salary, that move needs to solve a specific problem, not just generate excitement for two days.

And what about selling? Patrick Queen’s name has come up before. But a recognizable name doesn’t guarantee a strong return, and the replacement matters as much as what you get back. I don’t see it happening.

Why the picks matter.

Here’s the other part of the trade conversation that doesn’t get enough attention. Rodgers is 42, and this could be his final season. At some point, the Steelers need a long-term answer at quarterback, and the draft is where that’s most likely to come from. Drew Allar and Will Howard are developing, but nothing is settled.

That’s why I don’t want Khan throwing picks around just because the deadline is getting closer. Spend one when the return clearly helps. Keep it when the options don’t. Set a limit, and walk away if the price goes past it. A smart deadline is measured by what you add, not by how many moves you make.

The travel decision.

There’s one more decision that deserves attention, and this one belonged to McCarthy. According to KDKA Radio, the Saints left for France right after their Sunday night game, giving themselves almost a full week to adjust. The Steelers stayed home, practiced in Pittsburgh, and flew out after Thursday’s practice on an eight-hour charter, landing Friday morning. McCarthy said he chose that timing to keep preparation as normal as possible.

It’s a real gamble either way. More time in France means more time to adjust to the six-hour time difference. Less time means fewer distractions and a normal routine. We’ll find out on Sunday which approach paid off. But it’s a reminder that the big decisions this week were about preparation, not drama.

The Saints matchup.

Now let’s turn to Sunday. The Saints are 2-4, and they’ve had their own injury problems this week. Running back Alvin Kamara was limited with a back issue, linebacker Kaden Elliss was limited with a calf injury, and guard Cesar Ruiz didn’t practice. Quarterback Tyler Shough was a full participant despite a left hand injury.

And this is where the safety decisions matter again. The Saints have receivers who can make plays, including Chris Olave, and whoever starts at safety has to be disciplined in coverage. No assuming someone else has the assignment. Against a receiver like that, one miscommunication becomes a touchdown.

On the Steelers’ side, the injury report has some concerns. T.J. Watt was limited Thursday with a calf issue. Alex Highsmith was limited with his groin, and Rico Dowdle was limited with his knee. Jalen Ramsey upgraded to limited after missing Wednesday’s practice. Brandin Echols and Skowronek were ruled out.

Players already in the building.

That’s why the improvements already on this roster matter so much. Asante Samuel Jr. has looked better after some rough moments early in the season. Sean Murphy-Bunting gives the coaches a veteran option at corner. Nick Herbig had two sacks in Tampa, and if Watt or Highsmith is limited, he’ll be counted on even more. Could Herbig or Benton get at least a sack and a half against New Orleans? I can see it. But consistent pressure matters more than any single stat.

What about Boswell?

One more thing I want to address, because a lot of you brought it up. Chris Boswell missed a 48-yard field goal in Tampa, and that was frustrating. But let’s look at the whole day. He also made a 52-yarder before halftime, a 58-yarder late in the fourth quarter to tie the game, and the 20-yard go-ahead kick after Jamel Dean’s interception. One miss doesn’t define a performance like that. Boswell has been one of the most reliable kickers in football for a long time, and he came through when it counted.

Rodgers and the coaching staff.

And on offense, the Rodgers and McCarthy partnership is something to watch. After Tampa, Rodgers said, “Once we got DK involved, the offense just shifted. So we can get that going a little earlier next time.” That’s a veteran quarterback making a fair point. With an experienced quarterback like Rodgers, the coaching staff should give him room to adjust when something isn’t working. It doesn’t mean the coaches failed, and it doesn’t mean Rodgers should call everything. It means they have to recognize problems quickly and fix them during the game, not after it.

What’s at stake in Paris.

And let’s remember why all of these decisions matter so much this week. The Steelers are 4-2, tied with the Browns and Ravens atop the AFC North, with Cleveland holding first place on its division record. A win in Paris would make it three straight, and it would set up a home game against the Browns with first place on the line. A loss would turn that game into a must-win before the bye.

That’s the context for every move Khan made this week. Clearing space for Elliott, protecting the defensive line depth, adding a veteran receiver who knows the quarterback. None of it is flashy, but all of it is aimed at winning the next two games. And if the Steelers come out of this stretch at 6-2, the trade deadline conversation changes completely. Khan would be shopping as a division leader, with a clear picture of exactly what this roster needs.

The bottom line.

So did the Steelers’ owner make a shocking decision before the Saints game? No. Art Rooney II joined the safety conversations with the NFL, trusted the league’s plan, and flew to Paris to be there for a historic game. The real decisions came from the people he hired. Khan released Jenkins, moved Joseph-Day to injured reserve, brought back Valdes-Scantling, and cleared space for DeShon Elliott. And McCarthy and Graham now have to decide how to use all of it.

None of those moves are blockbusters. But together, they tell you this organization is trying to fix problems before they become crises. That’s a good sign heading into a game on the other side of the world, and an even better sign for the stretch that follows.
