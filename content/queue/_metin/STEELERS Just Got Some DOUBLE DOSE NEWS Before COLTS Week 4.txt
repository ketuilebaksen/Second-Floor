Now, let's talk about who Alex Highsmith actually is, because I think some fans take him for granted.

Highsmith was a third-round pick back in 2020 out of Charlotte. Not a first-rounder. Not a big-school star. A third-round project from a small program who turned himself into a legitimate starting edge rusher in the NFL. In July 2023, the Steelers rewarded him with a four-year, 68 million dollar extension that runs through the 2027 season. His cap hits are now north of 20 million dollars in both 2026 and 2027, according to reports. That number is going to matter in a minute.

And people forget how big his breakout was. In 2022, Highsmith exploded for 14 and a half sacks, playing a huge role while Watt missed time with a torn pec. He didn't just fill in. He carried the pass rush for long stretches. That season is a big reason the Steelers paid him the following summer. He proved he could be more than the guy on the other side of T.J. Watt. He proved he could be a problem by himself.

And he's producing. Through four games this year, Highsmith has three sacks, seven tackles, five of them for loss, five quarterback hits, and a pass defensed. Last season, he had nine and a half sacks in just 13 games, with 15 tackles for loss and 19 quarterback hits. For his career, he's now up to 48 sacks. That's not a guy who's slipping. That's a guy in his prime, playing at a high level, on a defense that desperately needs pass rush.

So why on earth would his name be on a trade list? Let me give you the three real reasons.

Reason number one is money and depth at the same position. Pittsburgh already pays T.J. Watt around 41 million dollars a year after his three-year, 123 million dollar extension last summer. Then, this past June, the Steelers handed Nick Herbig a four-year, 100 million dollar extension with 42 million guaranteed. That's 25 million a year for Herbig. So now you have three edge rushers who are all being paid like starters, and only two starting spots on the edge. Front offices look at that and ask whether they're spending too much in one room, especially when other rooms, like cornerback, are falling apart. According to one projection, moving Highsmith could save the Steelers around 14 and a half million in cap space for 2026. That's a lot of flexibility.

Reason number two is precedent. This front office just showed you it's willing to move a starter in the middle of the season if the business makes sense. Joey Porter Jr. went to Dallas for a 2028 second-round pick and a conditional sixth. Broderick Jones went to Dallas in August. George Pickens went to Dallas before that. When you've made those moves, insiders and rival general managers start assuming everybody is available for the right price. That's how names end up on lists.

Reason number three is the record. Pittsburgh is 2-2. The Browns are 3-1 and alone in first place in the AFC North. If the Steelers lose to the Colts and the Buccaneers in the next two weeks, they're 2-4, and suddenly the conversation inside the building shifts from "how do we win now" to "how do we set up next year." That's exactly the scenario where Highsmith becomes a real trade chip.

Okay, so what would he actually be worth? One projection put it this way: a team would likely need to give up a Day 2 pick and possibly more. Think a second or third-round selection, plus some kind of sweetener. That same projection mentioned the Lions and 49ers as teams that could be interested, but let me be very clear, that's a projection, not a report of real interest. Nobody has reported that Detroit or San Francisco has called about Highsmith. And Highsmith doesn't have a no-trade clause, so Pittsburgh would have total control over where he goes.

And if you want a real-world comparison for what edge rushers bring back at the deadline, look at Montez Sweat in 2023. Washington traded him to Chicago midseason for a second-round pick. That's the type of range we're talking about for a productive starter with term left on his contract. So when you hear "Day 2 pick," that's not a crazy number. It's roughly what the market has paid before. The question isn't whether Highsmith has value. He clearly does. The question is whether a second-round pick in the future is worth more to Pittsburgh than ten more games of a starting pass rusher right now.

Now let me give you the case for trading him, because I want to be fair before I tell you what I think.

If you're Omar Khan, you look at Herbig, 24 years old, just paid, already producing, and you see him as the long-term partner for Watt. You look at Highsmith, in the late stage of his twenties with two years left on his deal, and you know his trade value next offseason will be lower than it is right now. Every month that passes, he gets older and closer to free agency. If you're not sure you're winning a title this year, selling a little early can return more than selling a little late. And honestly, after watching the Porter situation, where the asking price fell from a 2027 first in the spring to a 2028 second in the fall, I understand why a front office would want to move before value drops.

Plus, the money you save could go straight into the secondary, which is the weakest unit on the team right now. Jamel Dean is in a walking boot with an ankle injury. Jalen Ramsey is playing with a broken wrist. Rookie Daylen Everette is learning on the fly. If trading a third edge rusher lets you add a real cornerback, there's a football argument for that.

But now here's the case for keeping him, and to me it's much stronger.

First, this is a win-now season. Aaron Rodgers announced in May that this is his last year. He said there's "zero debate." Mike McCarthy was hired to win immediately. You don't build around a 42-year-old quarterback in his final season and then start selling starters in October. That's a contradiction, and fans can see it.

Second, the pass rush isn't a luxury right now. It's a problem. Against Cleveland, Pittsburgh generated pressure on only about 21.6 percent of Watson's dropbacks, one of the lowest rates in the league that week. Derrick Harmon, who made the game-winning strip-sack against Cincinnati, was carted off with a foot injury and was in a walking boot, and we're still waiting on clarity about how long he'll be out. If you trade Highsmith now, you're taking a starting pass rusher away from a defense that already can't get home consistently and just lost its best young interior lineman. That doesn't fix anything. It makes it worse.

Third, depth on the edge isn't wasted money. It's insurance. T.J. Watt turns 32 this month. Edge rushers get banged up all the time. Last season and this season, the Steelers have been able to rotate fresh bodies and keep the pressure coming. Herbig and Highsmith both playing means Watt doesn't have to play every single snap, which keeps him fresher in the fourth quarter. Take Highsmith out of that rotation, and you're one injury away from serious trouble.

Fourth, think about the locker room. After the Browns loss, Highsmith was one of the guys who stood up and talked about accountability, saying there were missed tackles and that the group has to hold each other accountable. That's the kind of veteran voice you want in a room that just watched T.J. Watt grab a headset in frustration. Trading away respected veterans right after you trade away a popular young corner sends a message to that locker room, and it's not a good one.

So here's where I land on Highsmith, and this is my opinion, not a report. You keep him. Unless this team is 2-4 or worse heading into the deadline and the season is clearly slipping away, you don't sell a productive starting pass rusher in a win-now year. The Colts game on October 11th and the Tampa Bay game on October 18th are going to decide a lot. Win both, and this conversation dies. Lose both, and I'll be the first one to tell you the front office has to consider everything.

And history tells you the Steelers have usually been buyers when they believe, not sellers. Back in 2019, they traded a first-round pick to Miami for Minkah Fitzpatrick early in the season, and he became an All-Pro safety for them. That was a front office saying, "we believe in this group, let's go get help." The opposite move came in 2022, when they sold Chase Claypool to Chicago for a high second-round pick in a season that was going sideways. Two very different signals. So which version of the Steelers are we going to see in 2026? The buyer from 2019, or the seller from 2022? The answer to that question is basically the answer to the Highsmith question.

It's a mix of buying and selling, of win-now and build-later, and I think a lot of fans are confused about which direction this team is actually going.
