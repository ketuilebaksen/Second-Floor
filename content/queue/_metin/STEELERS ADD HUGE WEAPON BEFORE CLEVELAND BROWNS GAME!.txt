So yes, Pittsburgh beat Cincinnati 30-27. But the real story of this week might not be what happened on Sunday. It might be the decision this front office has to make before Thursday.

That's over seven yards a play, from an offense that could barely move the chains two weeks ago. They converted seven of twelve third downs. And the man at the center of that was Jaylen Warren. Seventeen carries, 127 yards. That's 7.5 yards every time he touched it on the ground. Add three catches for 49 more and you get 176 yards from scrimmage, a career high. And he did it on a shoulder that was so sore the day before that he wasn't catching passes in the walkthrough. Now let's talk about how the ball got spread around, because this is the part that impressed me most. DK Metcalf had three catches for 31 yards and the touchdown. Darnell Washington. Three catches, 67 yards, which ties his career high.

Think about that. A tight end who is built like an offensive lineman led the team in receiving yards. And it wasn't because Rodgers was throwing it sixty yards downfield to him. It was what happened after the catch. You've got a massive tight end who can move, run through arm tackles, and turn a five-yard throw into a twenty-yard gain. That's a different kind of problem for a defense. You can't cover him with a safety, and your linebackers don't want to tackle him in space.

Jaylen Warren, Pat Freiermuth, Ben Skowronek, Washington, Wilson, Metcalf. Nine different guys caught a pass. And I love that Pittsburgh didn't force the ball to Metcalf just because he's the name on the contract. The offense looked better when the ball went where the play design said it should go. And for the first time this year, they actually lived in the middle of the field. Routes into that space, Rodgers willing to throw it there, instead of the whole offense getting squeezed to the sideline.

Here's the chain reaction. The new right side, Brock Hoffman at guard and rookie Max Iheanachor at tackle, held up. According to PFF, Iheanachor allowed just three pressures on 36 pass-blocking snaps in his first NFL start. That was the lowest pressure rate on the entire line. The line holds, Rodgers gets time, Warren gets lanes, and suddenly the play calling can get aggressive. That's how you go from 16 points in two weeks to 30 in one game.

Okay. Now the other team. The one that showed up on defense.

Joe Burrow went 28 for 37, 282 yards, three touchdowns, no interceptions. Ja'Marr Chase caught nine balls for 98 yards and a score. Tee Higgins had six for 90 and a score. Cincinnati put up 352 total yards, and it honestly felt like they could move the ball whenever they needed to.

And the reason is pretty clear when you go back and watch. Pittsburgh played a lot of zone. The plan made sense on paper. Keep everything in front of you, don't let Chase or Higgins beat you over the top, make Cincinnati drive the length of the field. The problem is that Joe Burrow is perfectly happy to do exactly that. You give him eight-yard throws underneath, he'll take eight-yard throws all day long. He's not going to get impatient. He's going to march.

And zone defense only works if your pass rush makes the quarterback uncomfortable. That is the whole deal. You sit back in coverage, and your front four have to get home. On Sunday, for most of the afternoon, they didn't.

Here's the number that stunned me. T.J. Watt, according to PFF, finished with zero pressures. Zero. Cincinnati right tackle Amarius Mims had him handled most of the day. I'm not here to pile on T.J., he has been great through the first two weeks and he is still one of the best defensive players in football. But when your best pass rusher gets blanked, and you're playing that much zone, you're asking your secondary to cover forever. Nobody can do that against Chase and Higgins.

And the run defense wasn't dominant either. Chase Brown had 61 yards on 13 carries. Cincinnati ran 18 times for 83 yards. That's not a disaster, but it's not the Pittsburgh front we're used to seeing. Patrick Queen made a game-high 13 tackles, and a lot of those came after the ball carrier was already past the line.

Now look at the back end. Asante Samuel Jr. gave up the touchdowns to both Chase and Higgins, and eventually the Steelers pulled him for rookie Daylen Everette. I'll be fair to Samuel. On the Higgins touchdown he was right there. That was a great catch by a bigger receiver falling backward. Sometimes the coverage is good and the offense still wins. But when it happens twice in one game, the coaches make a change, and they did.

Here on Second Floor I want to be honest. This defense won the game with one play. It didn't win the game with sixty minutes.

But you can't count on a strip-sack in the final minute every week. And here's why this week is so dangerous.

The Monday injury report was an estimate, because it's a short week and Pittsburgh only held a walkthrough. Here's what we know. Jalen Ramsey has a wrist injury, and he was wearing a sling in the locker room. Mike McCarthy said it's too early to tell, but based on Ramsey's history, he thinks Jalen will do everything he possibly can to play. Brandin Echols is limited with a concussion. Rico Dowdle did not practice with the toe. The good news? Aaron Rodgers and Jaylen Warren weren't on the report at all. But look at that secondary again. Ramsey, hurt. Echols, dealing with a concussion. Samuel, benched on Sunday. Everette, a rookie. And you're heading to Cleveland on three days of rest.

Which brings us to Joey Porter Jr.

For the first time this season, Porter was listed as a full participant on the injury report, designated as conditioning. He hasn't played a single snap this year. He spent the summer on the PUP list with a back injury, and he's been inactive for three straight games.

And at the same time, the trade market is heating up. Adam Schefter reported on Sunday that a handful of teams, including the Dallas Cowboys and the Detroit Lions, have started asking about his availability. Porter is in the final year of his rookie contract. According to CBS Sports, the Steelers offered around 27 million dollars a year and he turned it down, looking for something closer to the top of the cornerback market. And Pittsburgh has a long-standing policy of not negotiating contracts once the regular season starts. Porter has said he never asked for a trade, and that he definitely sees himself playing this season. But you put all that together and you have a real standoff.

So let me give you both sides, because this is the biggest decision this front office will make before the deadline.

The case for trading him is simple. He's not playing. He doesn't have a new deal. Pittsburgh's policy says they won't negotiate right now. Teams like Dallas and Detroit have real cornerback needs and real motivation. If the offer is strong, you get premium draft capital for a player who might walk, or who might cost you a franchise tag after the season. That's cold, but that's how front offices think.

The case for keeping him just got a lot stronger on Sunday. A starting corner got benched mid-game. Ramsey is in a sling. Echols has a concussion. Burrow just threw for 282 and three touchdowns against your zone. And when you don't trust your corners to hold up in man, you end up sitting in zone. What does Joey Porter Jr. do? He plays press man. He gets physical at the line. He lets you blitz. He lets you stop playing scared on the back end.

Here's my take, and this is opinion, not a report. If Porter is truly healthy, you don't trade the one cornerback on your roster who changes your entire defensive identity in the same week your secondary is falling apart. You find a way to get him on the field. The contract fight is real, and I'm not pretending it goes away. But a 2-1 team in a division where everybody is 2-1 doesn't get better by shipping out its best press corner for picks. If this team wants to win now with a 42-year-old quarterback, the answer is probably on this roster, not in 2027 draft capital.

Now. Two things from Sunday that have to be fixed, no matter who's playing corner.

First, the clock. Pittsburgh burned all three first-half timeouts and two more in the second half. Five timeouts, a lot of them because they couldn't get the right personnel or the play in quickly enough. Some of that happens when an offense suddenly starts hitting big plays it isn't used to. But it can't keep happening. In a one-score game on the road, those timeouts are your last lifeline.

Second, special teams. Pittsburgh gave Cincinnati short fields too often in coverage. Yes, they got a massive gift when the Bengals muffed that punt and Skowronek recovered it. But you can't live on the other team's mistakes. Coverage units have to be better.
