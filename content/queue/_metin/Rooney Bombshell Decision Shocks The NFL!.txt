Steelers Nation, if you clicked on this video expecting Art Rooney II to have dropped some stunning announcement from the owner’s suite this morning, I owe you the truth right away. He didn’t. There is no statement from Rooney on the wire today. No press release. No quote about a trade. What there is, though, is a decision sitting on the desk of this organization that is about as “Rooney” as decisions get, because it’s a question of philosophy as much as football.

That decision has a name attached to it: Brandon Aiyuk.

Yes, that Brandon Aiyuk. The receiver Pittsburgh chased in the summer of 2024. The receiver who ended up re-signing in San Francisco instead. The receiver who hasn’t played a down in more than two years. This week he filed for reinstatement, he’s expected to report to the 49ers facility on Friday, and on national television this week, when a panel was asked who could use a player like him, one answer came out immediately: Pittsburgh.

So today we’re going to work through it the right way. What actually happened with Aiyuk this week. What San Francisco can do with him. What the rules say about timing. Why the Steelers’ ownership philosophy matters here. And finally, my board, opinion only, with the trade deadline arriving Tuesday, November 10, at 4 p.m. Eastern.

WHAT ACTUALLY HAPPENED THIS WEEK

Let’s lay out the facts first, because there’s been a lot of noise.

According to Pro Football Rumors, citing ESPN’s Adam Schefter, Brandon Aiyuk has been on the 49ers’ reserve/left squad list and has now filed paperwork to be reinstated. He’s expected to report to the team facility in Santa Clara on Friday, November 6.

His new agent, Drew Rosenhaus, whom Aiyuk hired earlier this week, put out a simple message: “Brandon is healthy and ready to get back on the field.”

Notice who’s saying that. The agent. That’s his job. An agent telling the world his client is healthy is not the same thing as a team doctor clearing him after a full physical. Any club interested in Aiyuk would need its own medical evaluation, period.

The transcript this script is based on also relays a second Rosenhaus line, laying out San Francisco’s choices as keeping him under contract, releasing him or trading him. I couldn’t find that exact wording in a published report, so treat it as a paraphrase. But it’s an accurate summary of where things stand, and it’s basically what every report says.

HOW WE GOT HERE

To understand why this is complicated, you need the backstory.

In August 2024, Aiyuk signed a four-year, one hundred twenty million dollar extension with San Francisco. That deal came together after a long, messy offseason in which Pittsburgh was heavily involved. At one point that summer, national reporting indicated the Steelers and 49ers had the framework of a trade in place. Then Aiyuk chose to stay, and he got paid.

Then came the injury. On October 20, 2024, in a loss to Kansas City, Aiyuk tore his ACL, his MCL and his meniscus in his right knee. That’s not a simple recovery. That’s one of the most serious knee injuries a receiver can suffer.

Last year, he spent most of the season on the reserve/PUP list. In July 2025, the 49ers voided the remaining guarantees in his contract. According to Pro Football Rumors, Aiyuk didn’t file a grievance within the fifty-day window, and the outlet estimates he lost about twenty-seven million dollars. In mid-December, the team moved him to the reserve/left squad list, where he hasn’t been paid.

The panel on that TV segment also took shots at Aiyuk’s behavior during the standoff, including social media videos and the way he openly lobbied to join Washington. I’ll keep it simple: that relationship has broken down, and San Francisco has made it clear it isn’t interested in repairing it.

WHAT KYLE SHANAHAN SAID

Speaking of which, here’s what the 49ers’ head coach said this week, according to Yahoo Sports.

Kyle Shanahan said he has “no expectation” of Aiyuk returning. He said he has “moved on with that mentally” and that “nothing’s really changed for me.” He also noted that Aiyuk “would have to show up” before anything could happen.

But here’s the nugget that keeps the door slightly ajar. When asked whether Aiyuk could ever play for San Francisco again this season, Shanahan said, “there’s no absolutes in life.”

That’s a coach being careful not to box himself in. It’s not a welcome mat. The 49ers are six and one and holding the top spot in the NFC. They have zero incentive to bring this situation back into their locker room in the middle of a run like that.

SAN FRANCISCO’S THREE OPTIONS

So what can the 49ers actually do? Yahoo lays out three paths.

Option one, the most likely: release him. Per Over the Cap numbers cited by Pro Football Rumors, cutting Aiyuk would create about seven point three six million dollars in dead money but free up about five point zero nine million in cap space this season. And San Francisco has more than seventy million dollars in cap room, the most in the league, so the dead money isn’t a real obstacle.

Option two: a trade for a small conditional pick. Yahoo mentions something like a 2027 sixth- or seventh-rounder. The problem is finding a team willing to take on the contract and the uncertainty at the same time.

Option three, the least likely: actually reinstate him and put him back on the field in red and gold.

And here’s the honest headline that cuts through a lot of the hype. Yahoo reports that no team is currently known to be actively pursuing a trade for him. The Steelers have not been reported as making a call. When that TV panel said “Pittsburgh,” it was analysts naming a logical fit, not insiders reporting a negotiation.

THE TIMING TRAP NOBODY IS TALKING ABOUT

Now, here’s the part I find most interesting, and it’s where timing could matter a lot for Pittsburgh.

Under the league’s waiver rules, as they’re generally understood, a vested veteran released before the trade deadline becomes a free agent right away and can sign anywhere. A player released after the deadline goes through waivers first, and waiver priority runs in reverse order of the standings. Aiyuk entered the league in 2020, so he should qualify as a vested veteran.

Why does that matter for the Steelers? Because at six and two, Pittsburgh would sit near the bottom of the waiver order. If San Francisco cuts Aiyuk after Tuesday, a struggling team would get first crack at him. If they cut him before Tuesday, every team, including Pittsburgh, could compete for him on the open market, and his agent would steer him to the situation he likes best.

So if the Steelers had any real interest, the smartest version of this story isn’t a trade. It’s a release before the deadline followed by a short, cheap, prove-it contract. That’s a big “if,” and nothing has been reported. But that’s the window to watch.

WHY THE FIT MAKES SENSE ON PAPER

Okay, let’s talk about why anyone connects these dots in the first place.

The last time Aiyuk was fully healthy, in 2023, he was one of the best receivers in football. Seventy-five catches. One thousand three hundred forty-two yards. Seven touchdowns. Second-team All-Pro. He averaged nearly eighteen yards a catch that season.

His game was built on precision. Clean releases, sharp breaks on intermediate routes, and the ability to turn a ten-yard catch into a thirty-yard play. That’s exactly the kind of receiver the Steelers have struggled to find this year.

And the need is real. Michael Pittman Jr. is on injured reserve with a foot injury, and he’s played in only three games this season. According to Steelers.com’s Asked and Answered, DK Metcalf came into the Browns game with a team-high 58 targets, then caught just one pass for four yards on six targets against Cleveland. The passing game, by the offensive coordinator’s own admission, has been out of sync on timing.

On that TV panel, one analyst pointed out that Rodgers has been completing a lot of passes close to the line of scrimmage without many chunk plays through the air. Another brushed that off, saying the Steelers are six and two with a defense that can hit you in the mouth. Both things can be true. A defense can carry you in October. In January, you usually need a receiver who can win when it matters.

There’s also a Rodgers angle. The panel’s take was that Rodgers would work with anybody talented enough to help, and that he might even be lobbying for help at receiver. That’s speculation from the panel, not a reported quote from Rodgers. But we do know Rodgers has been honest about the offense. After the Browns win, he told reporters: “Defense played excellent. Special teams did fine. But offense should have had a lot more points.”

THE RED FLAGS YOU CAN’T IGNORE

Now let’s be equally honest about the risks, because they’re serious.

First, the knee. Aiyuk hasn’t played a regular-season snap since October 20, 2024. That’s more than two years. Coming back from an ACL, MCL and meniscus tear is hard enough after a year. After two years away from game speed, the question isn’t just whether he’s healthy. It’s whether he still has the burst that made him special.

Second, conditioning and timing. Even if he’s cleared, he hasn’t practiced with an NFL team in a long time. Routes are about rhythm. Getting in sync with a quarterback who demands precision, like Rodgers, takes reps. The Steelers would be adding him midseason, with no training camp to build chemistry.

Third, the locker room. The way things ended in San Francisco has raised questions about fit and commitment. I’m not going to pile on him, but any front office would have to do its homework on the person, not just the player.

Fourth, the contract. If a team trades for him, it inherits his existing deal, which runs through 2028. With the guarantees voided, it’s more flexible than it looks, but it’s still a contract that would need careful handling. That’s another reason a release-and-sign route would be far more attractive than a trade.

WHERE ROONEY COMES IN

Now, let’s get to the “Rooney” part of this story, and why it’s actually a fair angle even without a bombshell quote.

In Pittsburgh, the owner sets the philosophy. Omar Khan runs the football operation, and Mike McCarthy coaches the team, but big swings, the ones involving real money, real risk and real attention, reflect the organization’s values from the top.

So what are Rooney’s values right now? Back in January, after the coaching change, Art Rooney II was asked about the idea of rebuilding. Here’s what he said, according to Pro Football Talk: “I don’t like that word that much. We’ll try to compete Day One if we can.” And then: “I’m not sure why you waste a year of your life not trying to contend.” And finally: “The standard is try to compete to win a championship every year.”

That’s a man who wants to win now. And at six and two in first place, with Rodgers in what is very likely his final chapter, “now” is exactly when this team should be looking for an edge.

But there’s another side to the Rooney philosophy that’s just as important: stability. The Steelers don’t typically chase drama. They don’t usually pay premium prices for players with question marks. They value culture, and they value people who fit it.

So the “Rooney decision” here isn’t about whether to make a splash for the sake of it. It’s about whether a potentially elite receiver, on a cheap, low-risk deal, is worth the uncertainty for a team trying to win a title. That’s a real organizational choice, and it’s one that would go beyond just the general manager’s office.

HOW THE STEELERS SHOULD THINK ABOUT IT

Let me frame this like a front office would.

If Aiyuk is released before Tuesday and Pittsburgh can sign him to a short, cheap, mostly incentive-based deal after a full medical, it’s close to a no-risk move. If he can’t play, you release him and lose almost nothing. If he can play at even seventy percent of his 2023 level, you’ve added a legitimate weapon for the stretch run.

If the only path is a trade that requires taking on his current contract, the math changes. Then you’re paying for uncertainty, and you’re giving up a pick on top of it.

And if he goes through waivers after the deadline, Pittsburgh probably never gets a real shot, and the decision is made for them.

Also, remember the context around the rest of this deadline. ESPN’s Jeremy Fowler said this week, “I don’t expect Pittsburgh to move anyone,” meaning he doesn’t expect the Steelers to sell. Buying is a different question, and players like Darnell Mooney, a one-year receiver ESPN’s Dan Graziano named as possibly available, remain cheaper and simpler alternatives.

THE SECOND-HALF PICTURE

Before the board, a quick reminder of where this team stands, because it frames how aggressive the Steelers should be.

Pittsburgh is six and two and first in the AFC North. On that same national TV segment, the Steelers made their first appearance in that show’s power rankings, at number nine. The analyst said that when he asked Cam Heyward who the team’s under-the-radar MVP was, Heyward answered immediately: Jaylen Warren. I couldn’t find that conversation in print, so take it as the analyst’s account. But the numbers back it up. Warren has 998 yards from scrimmage, second in the NFL, per Steelers.com.

The next game is November 15 in Cincinnati. The Bengals are five and two, the closest team chasing the Steelers. Pittsburgh won the first meeting back in Week 3. That’s the game a new receiver, any new receiver, would be preparing for.

WHAT A REALISTIC ROLE WOULD LOOK LIKE

Let’s say, hypothetically, everything breaks Pittsburgh’s way. Aiyuk gets released before Tuesday, passes a physical and signs a cheap deal. What would he actually be in November?

Not a savior on day one. That’s the honest answer. The most realistic plan would be to ease him in with a limited snap count, maybe fifteen or twenty plays a game at first, mostly on the intermediate routes he ran so well in San Francisco: digs, comebacks and deep outs where timing and footwork matter more than pure top speed. If the knee holds up and the rhythm comes back, you expand the role in December, when the games that decide seeding are played.

That approach protects the player, and it protects the offense. Nobody has to rebuild the game plan around a guy who’s been away for two years. He just becomes one more problem for a defense that already has to deal with Warren on the ground and Metcalf on the outside.

THE LESSON FROM 2024

There’s one more thing worth remembering. In 2024, the Steelers spent a whole summer in the Aiyuk saga, and in the end, he took the money in San Francisco. Pittsburgh moved on, eventually traded for Metcalf, and built the team that’s in first place today.

That history cuts both ways. On one hand, it shows the Steelers liked this player enough to seriously pursue him once. On the other, it’s a reminder that this front office has already shown it’s willing to walk away when the terms don’t make sense. That discipline is a big part of why the team isn’t desperate right now. And teams that aren’t desperate make better decisions at the deadline.

MY TRADE BLOCK BOARD (OPINION, NOT A REPORT)

Here’s my board. This is opinion, not a report. Nothing here is being described as an active negotiation.

Brandon Aiyuk, by trade, taking on his current contract: no. Too many unknowns, and I’m not spending a pick on a player who hasn’t played in two years.

Brandon Aiyuk, if released before the deadline: yes, on a one-year, low-base, incentive-heavy deal, only after a full medical. Low risk, potentially high reward. If the doctors aren’t comfortable, you walk away.

Darnell Mooney: still yes at a late Day 3 price. He’s healthy, he’s playing and he’s on a one-year deal. Honestly, he’s the safer move, and you could argue for doing both.

Mike Sainristil: yes at a Day 3 price for slot cornerback depth. A fifth-round pick is fair. Pittsburgh doesn’t own its 2027 fourth-rounder, so anything higher would require creative pick swaps.

Core players: off the table. Highsmith, Watt, Herbig, Queen, Warren, Frazier. A first-place team doesn’t sell its foundation in November.

The question now is whether a two-years-removed, once-elite receiver fits that standard, or whether the Steelers stick with the safer options and trust the team that got them to six and two.
