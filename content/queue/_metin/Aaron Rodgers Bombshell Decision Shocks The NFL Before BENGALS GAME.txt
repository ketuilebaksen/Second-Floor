Steelers Nation, the deadline came and went, and the most important decision hanging over this team this week wasn’t made on Tuesday afternoon. It was made back in May, by Aaron Rodgers, in three words.

“This is it.”

That’s what Rodgers said on May 20, two days after he signed his one-year deal to return to Pittsburgh, when he was asked whether 2026 would be his final season. Twenty-two seasons, and he has said there won’t be a twenty-third. No new announcement this week, no surprise retirement, no change of heart. But after what happened, or didn’t happen, at 4 p.m. Eastern on Tuesday, that May decision suddenly feels like the biggest story around this franchise. Because every choice Omar Khan made at the deadline now gets measured against one question: did the Steelers do enough for a quarterback who has told the world this is his last ride?

And on Sunday night, that last ride takes him to Cincinnati for a first-place showdown against Joe Burrow.

So today we’re going to cover it all. What the Steelers did, and didn’t do, at the deadline, and where Josh Downs ended up. Why Rodgers’ final-season decision makes that so meaningful. A warning from two former Steelers defenders about what Burrow’s offense is about to throw at this team. And why a rookie receiver might be the most important piece of the Rodgers puzzle on Sunday night. Then my post-deadline board, opinion only.

THE DEADLINE: STEELERS STAND PAT

Let’s start with the facts.

The Steelers made no trades before Tuesday’s deadline. According to SB Nation’s Joey Bray, as summarized by Yahoo Sports, Pittsburgh was one of only two AFC teams with six or more wins to make no moves at all, despite clear needs at receiver and cornerback.

And the name everyone in Pittsburgh was watching went somewhere else. Josh Downs, the Colts receiver the Steelers had been linked to for two weeks, was traded to the Kansas City Chiefs.

Remember, this was always the risk. Back on October 30, reporter Evan Sidery wrote that “The Chiefs and Steelers have both offered Day 3 draft compensation to the Colts for Josh Downs.” Pro Football Talk’s Mike Florio had described Downs as Kansas City’s “Plan B” if it couldn’t land Brian Thomas Jr. In the end, the Chiefs got their guy. Pittsburgh did not.

DK Pittsburgh Sports reported that the Steelers pursued two receivers before standing pat. The names weren’t specified in what I was able to access, but the reporting describes “a reluctance to make moves that wouldn’t benefit them beyond 2026.” That phrase is key. It tells you the front office wasn’t willing to spend future assets on players who might only help for ten weeks.

As for roster additions, Steelers On SI noted that the only moves of note were two defensive backs signed off other teams’ practice squads, plus veteran receiver Marquez Valdes-Scantling, who joined the practice squad back in October after the Chargers released him. Valdes-Scantling, of course, has a long history with Rodgers and McCarthy from their Green Bay days.

THE REACTION: NOT KIND

The grades were harsh.

Andrew Vasquez of Steelers Wire, writing on Yahoo Sports, gave Pittsburgh a D-minus. His summary: “It felt like Khan dropped the ball here.” He specifically called it a missed chance to support Rodgers in what is expected to be his final season, pointing to a thin receiver room, Metcalf’s issues with routine catches, and injuries to Michael Pittman Jr. and Roman Wilson. He did acknowledge that the Steelers are reluctant to spend draft capital while their long-term quarterback plan is unsettled, but he called the lack of even one modest move “extremely disappointing.”

Jacob Punturi of Steelers On SI was even blunter. “Yet in the most Steelers fashion ever, the team did nothing,” he wrote. And he went further: “Despite that, their inaction at the deadline proves that they are not legitimate Super Bowl contenders.” He contrasted Pittsburgh with Buffalo and Jacksonville, both of which made upgrades. Buffalo traded for Amani Hooker, the league’s interception leader, from Tennessee. Jacksonville added safety Jessie Bates and defensive tackles Davon Godchaux and Jordan Davis.

WHY DOWNS LANDING IN KANSAS CITY HURTS TWICE

Let’s sit with the Downs news for a second, because where he went matters almost as much as the fact that he didn’t come here.

Kansas City is an AFC contender. If the Steelers are going to make the kind of January run Rodgers came back for, there’s a real chance the road goes through Arrowhead at some point. And now the Chiefs have a quick, slot receiver who gets open underneath, exactly the kind of player Pittsburgh’s defense would have to account for on a third down in a playoff game.

That’s the double sting. Pittsburgh didn’t get better at receiver, and a team it might have to beat did. It doesn’t mean the Steelers were wrong to walk away at the price. But it does raise the stakes on the players already in the building. Bernard, Metcalf and the tight ends now have to be good enough on their own.

THE CASE FOR STANDING PAT

Now, let me give you the other side, because I think it deserves a fair hearing.

First, the price. The Steelers’ own 2027 fourth-round pick already belonged to Dallas from the Broderick Jones trade. Matching what the Colts wanted for Downs meant using a 2028 pick or overpaying in another way. And Downs was a rental, a player on the last year of his rookie deal who could walk in March.

Second, the quarterback future. If Rodgers retires after this season, and he has said he will, Pittsburgh needs every premium pick it can get to find his successor. Spending Day 2 capital on a receiver like Brian Thomas Jr. would have made that search harder.

Third, the fit. Gerry Dulac of the Post-Gazette pointed out that Downs, at five-foot-nine and 171 pounds, “does not appear to be the type of receiver Mike McCarthy appears to favor.” He wrote that “The Steelers coach prefers big receivers who are built to play in the cold-weather climates in the northeast.”

And fourth, the record. This team is six and two. It’s won four straight. The front office clearly believes in what’s already in the building.

WHERE I LAND ON THE DEADLINE

Here’s my honest take. I understand the logic, but I think this one stings. Not because the Steelers needed a blockbuster. They didn’t. But because a modest move, something at a Day 3 price that gave Rodgers one more reliable target, would have been a reasonable way to honor what Rodgers said in May.

When a quarterback tells you this is his last season, the window isn’t next year. It’s now. And while I respect the discipline, I would have liked to see the front office find one more piece.

That said, the deadline is over. The roster is set. And the only thing that matters now is what this team does with it, starting Sunday night in Cincinnati.

THE DECISION THAT FRAMES EVERYTHING: “THIS IS IT”

Let’s go back to that May press conference, because I don’t think people fully appreciate how much it shapes the rest of this season.

According to the Associated Press, Rodgers signed his one-year deal on Monday, May 18, and two days later he was asked if this would be his last year. “This is it,” he said. He didn’t elaborate on the decision. But he pointed to McCarthy, his coach for thirteen seasons in Green Bay, as a major factor in coming back. The two won a Super Bowl together after the 2010 season. The AP framed it as Rodgers wanting to leave the game on his own terms.

So think about what that means for every game from here on out. Every Sunday is one fewer chance. Every playoff run is the last one. And every divisional showdown, like the one coming this weekend, carries extra weight for a forty-two-year-old who has nothing left to prove except whether he can go out on top.

That’s why the deadline reaction was so emotional. It’s not about Downs. It’s about Rodgers.

THE WARNING: TWO FORMER STEELERS SOUND THE ALARM

Now let’s get to Sunday night, because the transcript this video is built on includes a conversation that every Steelers fan should hear.

According to the transcript, it’s former Steelers linebacker James Harrison and former Steelers cornerback Joe Haden talking through the Bengals matchup. I’m paraphrasing and cleaning up the language, but the message was crystal clear. You can’t stop this offense. You can only slow it down. And if you can’t slow it down, it’s going to be a long night.

Harrison, according to the transcript, called it a true measuring stick for this defense. He said the front seven has to be dominant and in control, the pass rush has to hit and pressure Burrow all game, and the rush and coverage have to work together. He also said something every defensive coach in the league would agree with: somebody has to stop somebody.

And he made a point about the first meeting that matters. In his view, the Bengals came to Pittsburgh in September thinking it would be easy, and they found out it wasn’t. But he warned that the Steelers will be facing a different team this time.

THE NUMBERS BEHIND THE WARNING

The transcript includes a set of numbers to back that up. I couldn’t independently confirm every one of them, so treat these as reported.

According to the transcript, Cincinnati’s offense has scored at least thirty-one points in four straight games, and its passing offense ranks first in the league at about 286 yards per game. Its rushing offense ranks near the bottom, around thirtieth. On the other side, the Bengals’ defense is described as one of the league’s weakest, ranked twenty-fifth in total yards allowed and dead last in rushing yards allowed per play.

For Pittsburgh, the transcript describes the defense as seventh overall, third against the run and sixteenth against the pass.

Here’s what I can confirm. In Madrid on Sunday, Burrow completed 24 of 29 passes, 82.8 percent, for 271 yards and three touchdowns with no interceptions, plus a rushing touchdown, in a 34-17 win over Atlanta. Ja’Marr Chase had ten catches for 155 yards. Tee Higgins caught two touchdowns. And a week before that, Burrow threw five touchdown passes in an overtime win over Tennessee, according to the Associated Press.

The transcript also says Chase and Higgins combined for 188 receiving yards in the first meeting with Pittsburgh, and that they’ve piled up 693 yards and eight touchdowns over their last three games. Again, reported, not confirmed by me. But it fits everything we’ve seen. This passing game is on fire.

THE ZONE COVERAGE DETAIL THAT COULD DECIDE THE GAME

My favorite part of the Harrison and Haden conversation was a very specific technical point, and I want to break it down because it’s the kind of thing that decides big games.

According to the transcript, Haden described something he saw Chase do repeatedly in Madrid. A linebacker drops into his zone and gets to his spot. Chase sees him there and, instead of running right into that defender, he drifts a couple of steps away, bumping just outside the linebacker’s reach. Burrow already knows where Chase is going to be, so he throws it the moment Chase turns. And now the linebacker is three or four yards away, trying to close the gap while Chase is already running up the field.

Here’s the coaching point. In zone coverage, it’s not enough to just get to your spot. You have to relate to the receiver who’s entering your area. If you’re just standing in your zone, a smart route runner will find the soft edge of it. And Chase is one of the best in football at that.

So what does Pittsburgh need from Patrick Graham’s defense? Tight windows underneath. Linebackers who don’t just drop, but who drop and then attach to the nearest threat. And help over the top when Chase or Higgins goes deep.

The Bengals’ passing game, according to the transcript, lives on deep crossers, digs, corner routes and go balls when they see man coverage on the outside. Those are the throws where a quarterback and receiver who know each other perfectly can make a defense look bad.

THE PASS RUSH HAS TO TRAVEL

The other half of the equation is pressure.

The Steelers had eight sacks against Cleveland. T.J. Watt had two. Patrick Queen and Cam Heyward had a sack and a half each. Nick Herbig and rookie Kevin Jobity Jr. each had one.

That kind of disruption has to travel to Cincinnati. If Watt and the front can get Burrow off his spot before the deeper routes develop, the timing of that passing game breaks down. If they can’t, Burrow will pick them apart.

And there’s the run game. The transcript’s numbers suggest Cincinnati doesn’t run the ball well. That’s an opportunity. If the Steelers’ front four can control the run without help, the linebackers and safeties can focus on the passing game. Tackle the first guy. Get them into long down-and-distance. Make Burrow earn everything.

THE BEST DEFENSE: BURROW ON THE SIDELINE

Here’s a thought that the transcript raises, and I love it: what if the biggest play Pittsburgh makes against Burrow on Sunday night happens while Burrow is standing on the sideline?

In other words, the best way to slow down an elite quarterback is to keep the ball away from him. Long drives. Third-down conversions. Time of possession.

That’s where Jaylen Warren comes in. He’s second in the NFL with 998 scrimmage yards, according to Steelers.com. And if the transcript’s numbers on Cincinnati’s run defense are anywhere close to accurate, this is a matchup where Warren and Rico Dowdle could control the game.

But you can’t win time of possession with the run game alone. You need to convert third downs. And that’s where a rookie receiver enters the picture.

THE ROOKIE WHO COULD BE RODGERS’ BEST FRIEND

Let’s talk about Germie Bernard. One quick correction first: the transcript calls him “Jimmy Bernard.” It’s Germie.

The Steelers traded up to take him in the second round, forty-seventh overall, out of Alabama. And according to the transcript, offensive coordinator Brian Angelichio has talked about his ability to play inside or outside.

Here are the numbers the transcript attributes to Athlon Sports. Through eight games, Bernard has thirty-two catches for 284 yards. A hundred seventy-four of those yards came after the catch. He’s picked up fourteen first downs. And among Steelers receivers with at least fifty routes, he leads the team at 3.6 yards of separation per target. I couldn’t find the Athlon piece myself, so let’s treat those as reported, but they match what we’ve seen on the field.

What I can confirm is his clutch moment. Against Tampa Bay, Bernard had eleven catches for eighty-seven yards and caught the overtime touchdown that won the game. The transcript adds that it was a six-yard score and that McCarthy gave him a game ball. That’s the kind of play that tells you a quarterback trusts a young receiver.

And Rodgers has said as much. “There comes a time when the confidence starts to match the potential, and I think we’re reaching that time with Germie,” he said.

Now, there’s a fair counterpoint. Vasquez’s deadline grade pointed out that Bernard has managed only fifty-seven receiving yards over his last two games. Against Cleveland, he caught all five of his targets, but for only thirty yards. So the production has cooled off.

But here’s why I’m optimistic. With no trade, Bernard is the number two receiver for the rest of the season. That means more snaps, more targets and more chances to build on what he’s already shown. Picture it: third-and-six in Cincinnati, the crowd going crazy, and Rodgers hits Bernard on a quick throw that he turns into a first down. That’s the play that keeps Burrow on the sideline.

WHAT RODGERS DID THE FIRST TIME

Before we lay out the formula, it’s worth remembering how Rodgers handled this Bengals team in September, because it shows the blueprint.

In the 30-27 win on September 27, Rodgers completed 19 of 34 passes for 292 yards and three touchdowns, according to the Associated Press. The play that set up Chris Boswell’s winning 33-yard field goal was a 47-yard catch-and-run by tight end Darnell Washington. And it came a week after a rough loss to New England in which Rodgers was sacked four times.

After the game, Rodgers was asked about McCarthy’s message to “cut it loose,” and he laughed it off: “I feel like he says that every week.”

That’s the version of Rodgers the Steelers need on Sunday night. Aggressive, decisive and willing to push the ball downfield to his tight ends when the defense sells out to stop the run. Against a Cincinnati defense that, according to the transcript’s numbers, has struggled badly against the run, the play-action shots should be there.

THE OPPORTUNITY ON THE OTHER SIDE

And that’s the part of this matchup that doesn’t get talked about enough.

Everyone is focused on how Pittsburgh’s defense will handle Burrow. Fair enough. But if the transcript’s numbers are close, Cincinnati’s defense is the weaker unit in this game. A team that gives up yards on the ground and has to outscore people every week is a team that can be worn down.

So here’s the plan I’d want. Pound Warren and Dowdle early. Make the Bengals’ front prove it can stop the run. When the safeties start creeping toward the line, take the shots off play-action to Washington, Freiermuth or Metcalf. And when it’s third-and-medium, look for Bernard underneath.

If Pittsburgh can win the game on the ground and keep Burrow off the field, the best passing offense in the league becomes a lot less scary.

THE FORMULA FOR SUNDAY NIGHT

So let’s put it all together.

Metcalf pulls coverage outside. Warren makes defenders respect the run. Bernard works the space underneath. The tight ends, Pat Freiermuth and Darnell Washington, threaten the seams. And Rodgers gets the ball out on time.

On defense, the front four controls the run. Watt and company get Burrow off his spot. The linebackers relate to receivers in zone instead of just standing there. And the safeties keep Chase and Higgins in front of them.

Do that, and the Steelers have a real chance to sweep the Bengals and take sole possession of first place in the AFC North. Don’t do it, and, as Harrison put it, it’s going to be a long night.

WHY THIS GAME MEANS MORE FOR RODGERS

Let’s bring it back to the headline.

Rodgers has said this is his last season. He came to Pittsburgh for one more shot at a title with the coach who helped him win his first. And now, after a deadline where the front office chose not to add help, he’s heading into the biggest game of the season so far with the roster he has.

That’s not a complaint. It’s a challenge. If Rodgers can beat Burrow twice in one season, on the road, on national television, with this group of receivers, it’ll be one of the defining moments of his final chapter. And it’ll prove the front office right for trusting the guys in the building.

MY POST-DEADLINE BOARD (OPINION, NOT A REPORT)

The deadline is over, so here’s a different kind of board. Opinion, not a report.

Germie Bernard: give him more targets. He’s the number two now. Let him grow into it.

DK Metcalf: get him involved early with easy completions before asking him to win contested balls.

Marquez Valdes-Scantling: worth a look as a practice-squad elevation if the receiver room gets any thinner. He knows Rodgers. He knows McCarthy.

The secondary: no trade came, so the coaching has to carry it. Zone discipline against Chase is the whole ballgame.

The core: still untouchable. Watt, Highsmith, Herbig, Queen, Ramsey, Warren, Frazier.

Here’s my bottom line. The Aaron Rodgers decision that matters most wasn’t made this week. It was made in May, when he said, “This is it.” And that’s exactly why this week stings a little. A quarterback in his final season came back for one more run, and the front office decided the roster it already had was good enough.

Now we find out if they’re right. Sunday night in Cincinnati. Six and two against six and two. Rodgers against Burrow, one last time in the regular season. And a defense that two Steelers legends say is about to face its true measuring stick.
