Steelers Nation, Mike McCarthy didn’t spend Monday defending what happened in Cincinnati. He spent it admitting what’s broken, and telling us exactly what he plans to change.

That’s the real story behind the headline. There’s no firing and no benching. But McCarthy did say something you rarely hear from an offensive-minded head coach: that the core belief of his passing game isn’t working right now. He said the staff will re-examine its early-down play calls. He said the receivers need to catch the ball better. And he promised answers on two injured receivers before Wednesday’s practice.

The source for today’s video goes even further. It argues that the Steelers’ biggest problem isn’t depth or injuries. It’s the stars: Aaron Rodgers and DK Metcalf, the two players this offense was built around.

So today, we’re going to go through McCarthy’s decisions one by one, look at the numbers behind the transcript’s criticism of Rodgers and Metcalf, examine the defense, and talk about why some fans are already comparing this to 2021. My board at the end, opinion only.

Let’s get into it.

MCCARTHY’S SURPRISING ADMISSION

Let’s start with the quote that’s getting the most attention.

At his Monday press conference, according to Steeler Nation, McCarthy explained that the middle of the field is the foundation of how he wants to throw the football. “The fastest way to the end zone is through the middle of the field,” he said.

Then came the honest part. “But the stats and video right now don’t support that.”

Think about how rare that is. A head coach who calls plays telling reporters, two days after a blowout, that his favorite philosophy isn’t producing results. That’s not spin. That’s a coach looking at the film and accepting what it shows.

URGENT DECISION ONE: THE EARLY-DOWN CALLS

So what’s the fix? According to Steeler Nation, McCarthy said the staff would take a closer look at its first- and second-down play calls this week.

He also emphasized playing “ahead of the sticks.” In plain English, that means gaining enough on first and second down that third down is short and manageable. When an offense is ahead of the sticks, it can attack the middle of the field with quick throws and play action. When it’s behind, it’s forced into long third downs, and the defense knows a pass is coming.

Offensive coordinator Brian Angelichio echoed that, according to Steeler Nation, saying the offense needs to stack successful plays together and produce more chunk gains.

That’s the decision: rebuild the early-down plan so Rodgers isn’t standing in third-and-long all afternoon.

THE START THAT MADE HISTORY

To understand why McCarthy is focused on early downs, look at how Sunday began.

According to WPXI, Rodgers opened the game 0-for-8. It was the first time in his 22-year NFL career that he started a game that badly. Before Sunday, he had never even opened a game 0-for-5. WPXI also reported he became the fifth quarterback since 2000 to go 0-for-7 or worse across his team’s first three drives.

And it could have been worse. WPXI notes that Bengals safety Jordan Battle nearly intercepted an overthrow intended for Darnell Washington.

According to Steeler Nation, Pittsburgh went three-and-out on its first two possessions, and Rodgers didn’t complete a pass until late in the first half.

The transcript adds a painful comparison. It says that by the time Rodgers got his first completion, Joe Burrow had already completed 21 of 26 passes for 245 yards and two touchdowns. I couldn’t verify that exact split, so take it as the transcript’s number. But the full-game totals tell the same story: Burrow finished 35 of 44 for 409 yards and four touchdowns.

THREE QUARTERS OF NOTHING

If 0-for-8 sounds bad, the full three-quarter picture is worse.

According to Yardbarker, citing TruMedia, the Steelers had just 34 yards of offense through three quarters. They went 0-for-7 on third downs over that stretch, and six of those were third-and-long.

That’s the clearest evidence for McCarthy’s early-down focus. Third-and-long against a defense playing press-man coverage is about the hardest situation in football. And Pittsburgh lived there all night.

Jaylen Warren had only five touches in the first half, according to Yardbarker. At halftime, Darnell Washington had one catch and Pat Freiermuth had none. The players who make this offense work were barely involved.

McCarthy summed it up, according to Yardbarker: “We wanted to play a sort of style of game, but we never really got into it.”

THE TRANSCRIPT’S CRITICISM OF RODGERS

Here’s where the source video gets pointed, and I want to represent its argument fairly.

The host says the 0-for-8 start isn’t what worries him most. What worries him is how plays were developing. He acknowledges the pressure was real. But he also says that on snaps when Rodgers had time, the ball was often already gone: a quick dump-off, a throwaway, or a play that felt over before anything downfield had a chance.

His line is a good one: protecting the football can’t become the entire passing game. And he draws a distinction I think is fair. There’s a difference, he says, between a 42-year-old quarterback avoiding hits smartly and one who plays as if he expects a hit on every snap.

That’s the host’s opinion from watching the game. I haven’t seen tracking data that confirms it. But it lines up with Athlon’s midseason numbers, which ranked Rodgers 31st in average depth of target before this game. When a quarterback’s average throw is that short, defenses can crowd the line and take away the easy stuff.

RODGERS’ OWN EXPLANATION

Rodgers offered a different explanation, and it’s worth hearing.

According to ClutchPoints, when asked whether the Bengals changed their approach from the Week 3 meeting, Rodgers said: “They played press coverage, they played a lot of man coverage, and we didn’t make them pay at all.”

Notice what he’s saying. Press-man coverage is designed to take away timing throws and force receivers to win one-on-one. If the receivers don’t separate, the quarterback either holds the ball and takes a sack, or throws it away. Rodgers is suggesting the problem was the receivers not beating that coverage.

He didn’t name anyone, and ClutchPoints noted that his comments “came close” to criticizing the receivers without crossing the line. But it’s not hard to see who he meant.

THE PLAY-ACTION CLUE

Here’s a stat that might point to the real answer.

According to Yardbarker, citing analyst Tej Seth in an October 12 post, the Steelers’ play-action dropbacks ranked fourth in the league in EPA per play and ninth in success rate. Their non-play-action dropbacks ranked 30th and 32nd.

Read that again. When Pittsburgh fakes the handoff, the passing game has been one of the best in football. When it doesn’t, it’s been one of the worst. And yet, according to Yardbarker, the Steelers’ play-action rate is just over 19 percent.

Angelichio seems to know it. According to Yardbarker, he said the offense needs to attack the middle of the field more and that play action can help.

But here’s the catch: play action only works if you can run the ball, and you can only run the ball if you’re not trailing by three scores. That’s why the first-down plan matters so much. Stay on schedule, keep Warren involved, and the play-action game opens up.

URGENT DECISION TWO: THE METCALF PROBLEM

That brings us to DK Metcalf, and the numbers are tough.

On Sunday, according to Heavy, Metcalf was targeted six times and caught two passes for 54 yards and a touchdown, plus the two-point conversion. The transcript points out that all of it came in the fourth quarter, after the game was decided.

His season numbers through nine games, per Heavy: 35 catches on 70 targets, a 50 percent catch rate, for 432 yards and two touchdowns. That’s about 48 yards a game. Warren Sharp, cited by Heavy, puts Metcalf’s drop rate at 14.6 percent.

And Metcalf isn’t paid like a complementary piece. According to Heavy, he’s on a five-year, 150 million dollar deal, worth about 33 million a year in new money. The transcript makes the point bluntly: you pay a receiver that much because you expect him to change a game from the opening drive.

McCarthy addressed it directly. According to Heavy, citing Steelers Depot, he said: “We do need to catch the ball better, so we understand that.”

THE TRUST CYCLE

The transcript makes an observation about Rodgers and Metcalf that I think is the smartest part of the video.

It argues you can’t separate the two problems. Rodgers looks hesitant, and Metcalf isn’t consistently giving him a dependable target. So the quarterback gets more cautious. The receiver gets fewer chances to work through whatever’s bothering him. And the cycle feeds itself.

That’s how trust breaks down between a quarterback and a receiver. It rarely happens in one game. It happens over a few drops, a few late throws, a few routes where they read the coverage differently. And it rarely gets fixed in one game either.

The transcript’s prescription is simple: if Rodgers has time, Metcalf has to get open. If Metcalf gets open, Rodgers has to deliver. That’s the connection Pittsburgh paid for, and without it, the whole offense shrinks.

WHAT CINCINNATI HAD THAT PITTSBURGH DIDN’T

The contrast with the Bengals is hard to ignore.

Ja’Marr Chase caught 11 passes for 231 yards. Tee Higgins added five catches for 66 yards. Running back Chase Brown caught two touchdown passes. Cincinnati had multiple players demanding attention on every snap, so defenders couldn’t sit on one option.

That’s what the transcript says Pittsburgh needed from Metcalf: not a late touchdown to clean up the box score, but a steady threat from the first series. A receiver his size, the host argues, should be winning one-on-one matchups while the game is still in doubt.

URGENT DECISION THREE: THE RECEIVER RETURNS

The transcript also points out that the rest of the receiving group isn’t giving Rodgers much help, and that brings us to McCarthy’s third decision.

According to Steeler Nation, McCarthy said the team would have answers on Roman Wilson and Michael Pittman Jr., both on injured reserve, before Wednesday’s practice. He’d already said he was “hopeful” Wilson could play in Philadelphia, according to PennLive, as reported by CBS Sports.

That matters because of rookie Germie Bernard. The transcript calls him “Jeremy,” but it’s Germie. The host’s point is fair: many fans wanted Bernard to become a major option right away, but expecting a rookie to carry the passing game is asking too much. A young receiver develops faster when the veterans around him are producing.

Getting Wilson and Pittman back wouldn’t fix everything. But it would give Rodgers more targets, and it would take pressure off both Metcalf and Bernard.

THE TIGHT ENDS AND THE MIDDLE OF THE FIELD

Here’s a connection worth making.

If McCarthy wants to get back to attacking the middle of the field, the tight ends are the obvious answer. According to the transcript, Pat Freiermuth finished with two catches for 35 yards and a touchdown, all late. Darnell Washington was involved too, including on the overthrow that Battle nearly picked off.

The transcript says both tight ends need to be involved more consistently, and I agree. Tight ends are built for the middle of the field. They’re bigger than the linebackers and safeties covering them, and they give a cautious quarterback a target that’s usually open early in the play.

So if you want a hint about how the early-down changes might look, watch for more tight end targets on first and second down in Philadelphia.

URGENT DECISION FOUR: THE RUN GAME AND THE LINE

The fourth decision is up front.

According to the transcript, Jaylen Warren had just 15 rushing yards on seven carries, plus five catches for 26 yards. Those numbers add up to the 41 yards from scrimmage the AP reported. For one of the league’s most productive backs, that’s nowhere near enough.

Part of that was falling behind 17-0. But part of it was the line, and now the line has a new problem. Left guard Mason McCormick was carted off with a right ankle injury and still has no diagnosis. Spencer Anderson is expected to start in his place, and the team was set to decide whether to open rookie Gennings Dunker’s practice window before Wednesday’s practice, according to Athlon.

The transcript makes a good point here, too. The line has to block better, but it can’t explain every problem. There were snaps when Rodgers had time and didn’t make enough of it. The blockers, the quarterback and the receivers all have to take advantage when protection holds.

THE DEFENSE DOESN’T GET A PASS

The transcript is just as hard on the defense, and the numbers back it up.

Cincinnati put up 484 yards, according to Heavy. Pittsburgh sacked Burrow only once, according to Steelers.com. The transcript says there wasn’t enough pressure, and that short-yardage situations weren’t handled well.

The host also names names in the secondary. He says Asante Samuel Jr. struggled against strong competition, and that concerns around safety Jaquan Brisker were impossible to ignore. Those are the host’s evaluations from the film, and I’d treat them as opinion.

And on linebacker Payton Wilson, the transcript makes an interesting point. Wilson piled up 17 tackles, according to Steelers.com, but the host says he still had problems in coverage and tackling. That’s the danger of a big tackle number: sometimes it means a player was making stops downfield after the damage was done.

Defensive coordinator Patrick Graham’s message this week, according to Steeler Nation, was: “Our toughness didn’t go away because we got beat.” Now the defense has to prove it.

HOW THE NATIONAL MEDIA REACTED

The national power rankings were mixed, according to a Yahoo Sports roundup published Tuesday.

Pro Football Talk’s Mike Florio dropped the Steelers from seventh to 11th, writing that they’re “not ready to compete with the best teams in the conference.” FOX Sports’ Ralph Vacchiano moved them from 12th to 15th and called it “a good, old-fashioned butt-kicking.” CBS Sports’ Pete Prisco dropped them one spot, to 10th, noting that the defense “was shredded by Joe Burrow.”

But several outlets kept them in place. ESPN’s Brooke Pryor left them at 13th and called Warren “the Steelers’ most reliable option to move the ball.” Yahoo’s Frank Schwab also kept them at 13th, but added: “One thing that is an issue for the Steelers is their receiver room.”

And USA Today’s Nate Davis pointed out an embarrassing detail. Pittsburgh became “the first team coming off a bye week to lose to an opponent coming back from a game in Europe.” Remember, Cincinnati had just played in Madrid.

WHY FANS ARE THINKING ABOUT 2021

Now, the transcript brings up a comparison that a lot of longtime fans have been making.

In 2021, the Steelers went to Cincinnati and lost 41-10. They still made the playoffs, and they drew Kansas City in the wild-card round. Before that game, Mike Tomlin delivered one of the most memorable lines of his tenure. According to Fox News, he told his team: “If you’re a blinker, cut your eyelids off.”

Then Kansas City won 42-21, according to the AP.

The transcript’s lesson from that season is sharp: a good record can hide real flaws until a strong opponent finds them. The host says he doesn’t want another season that feels like a rerun, and I think that’s the fear behind all the reaction this week.

WHY 2026 CAN STILL BE DIFFERENT

But the transcript doesn’t write this team off, and neither do I.

The Steelers are still 6-3. The AFC is open. And as the host points out, the next two games, at Philadelphia and home against Denver, are real tests that could change the conversation fast. Winning both, especially the Denver game, would put Pittsburgh in a much better position.

The host also paints the optimistic picture. Imagine this offense with a passing game that threatens defenses for four quarters, with Rodgers throwing with confidence and Metcalf playing like an elite receiver again. Then defenses can’t focus on one thing. That’s exactly what made Cincinnati so hard to stop on Sunday.

The gap between what this team is showing and what it could be is real. But so is the talent. The question is whether McCarthy’s adjustments can close that gap.

THE SCHEDULE WORRY

There’s one more reason this week matters, and it’s about the rest of the season.

According to Yardbarker, Cincinnati’s remaining schedule includes only two opponents currently over .500: the Browns, twice, and the Chiefs. Pittsburgh’s road includes the Jaguars, the Broncos and the Ravens twice, possibly with Lamar Jackson back.

So the Steelers can’t count on the Bengals stumbling. If Pittsburgh wants to win the division, it probably needs to win more games than Cincinnati down the stretch against a tougher schedule. That raises the stakes on every game, starting with the Eagles.

PHILADELPHIA: THE FIRST TEST OF THE CHANGES

Here’s what’s next.

The Steelers visit the Eagles on Sunday, November 22, at 1 p.m. Eastern on CBS, after the game was flexed from 4:25, according to Steelers Now. Philadelphia is 2-7 and has lost seven straight, according to Yahoo Sports. The Eagles are coming off their bye and will start Joe Milton at quarterback.

And the history is not on Pittsburgh’s side. The Steelers haven’t won a regular-season game in Philadelphia since 1965, according to Steelers.com.

The transcript says it will be watching the early drives closely, because that’s where we’ll learn whether the lessons from Cincinnati stuck. That’s exactly right. McCarthy told us he’s re-examining first and second down. Philadelphia’s first two or three possessions will show us whether that work paid off.

WHAT TO WATCH ON THE OPENING DRIVES

So here’s my checklist for those first drives.

First, the first-down calls. Are the Steelers gaining four or five yards on first down, or are they behind the sticks right away?

Second, the middle of the field. Are Freiermuth and Washington getting early targets? Are there quick in-breaking routes for Metcalf?

Third, Rodgers’ timing. When he has a clean pocket, is he letting plays develop, or is the ball coming out before routes finish?

Fourth, Warren. Does he get the ball early and often, even if the first carries don’t work?

Fifth, the new left guard. Does the left side hold up when the Eagles test it?

If most of those boxes are checked, the adjustments are working. If not, the questions from Cincinnati will only get louder.

MY BOARD (OPINION, NOT A REPORT)

Here’s my board for the week. Remember, this is opinion, not a report.

Early downs: more quick passes and play action on first down. Get Rodgers a completion on the first play from scrimmage.

Middle of the field: script at least three early targets to the tight ends. Make the linebackers cover them.

Metcalf: simple, catchable routes early. Slants and digs, not deep shots. Let him build confidence before you push the ball downfield.

Receivers: activate Roman Wilson if he practices fully. He gives the offense a different look against press coverage.

Run game: 20 or more carries for Warren and Rico Dowdle combined, no matter what happens early.

Defense: more pressure on the young quarterback, and tighter coverage over the middle where Payton Wilson and the safeties struggled.

FINAL WORD

Here’s my bottom line. McCarthy didn’t make a dramatic move this week. He did something harder: he admitted his offense isn’t doing what he believes in, and he committed to fixing it.

The early-down calls are getting a fresh look. The receivers have been told to catch the ball better. Two injured receivers could return. And a new left guard is stepping in.

Now it’s on Rodgers, Metcalf and the rest of the stars to show that Cincinnati was a bad night, not a preview of December. Philadelphia is where we find out.
