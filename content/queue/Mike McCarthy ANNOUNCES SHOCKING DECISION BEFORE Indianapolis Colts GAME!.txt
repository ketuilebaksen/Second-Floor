Steelers Nation, here's a detail from last Sunday that nobody in Pittsburgh is talking about, and I think it tells the whole story of this week. In Dallas, Joey Porter Jr. played his first game for the Cowboys. Limited snaps, but he lined up against Nico Collins several times and Collins didn't get a single target on those snaps. On the other sideline, in that same game, a young Texans cornerback had the roughest afternoon of any corner in the NFL this season. That corner's name is Kamari Lassiter, and his name is now showing up on lists of players the Steelers could trade for. Think about that. The guy Pittsburgh let go shut down his matchup, and the guy Pittsburgh might chase got torched, on the same field, on the same day. So today, I want to ask the real question behind all of this week's moves. Is Omar Khan executing a plan, or is this front office just reacting? We're going to go piece by piece: the Porter money, the Murphy-Bunting signing, the hole Derrick Harmon left, a familiar linebacker still sitting on the market, and that Lassiter idea. This is James with Second Floor, let's get into it.

Quick housekeeping first, because a few versions of this story going around this week are a little sloppy. Sean Murphy-Bunting is a cornerback. He's insurance for the secondary, not for the quarterback room. Porter played 24 snaps in his Dallas debut, 19 of them in coverage, so yes, the debut was promising, but it was a limited workload. And the trade return everybody keeps quoting is correct: Dallas sent Pittsburgh a 2028 second-round pick and a 2027 sixth-rounder. Keep that price in mind, because it's going to matter when we get to Lassiter.

Piece number one: why Porter is in Dallas in the first place.

This is the foundation of everything, so let's be precise. According to Ian Rapoport, the Steelers offered Porter a deal worth around 28 million dollars a year. That's real money. But Pittsburgh held firm on one thing: no injury guarantees beyond the first year. And that's not a Porter-specific decision. That's a franchise philosophy. Pittsburgh, like Green Bay and Cincinnati, generally avoids guaranteeing money in the second year of a contract. The notable exception is T.J. Watt, and when you're T.J. Watt, you get exceptions.

Porter, meanwhile, wanted security. He wanted a long-term deal with protection, not a big number that could vanish after one season. Add in the back injury, the fact that he sat out training camp, and GM Omar Khan saying there would be "no negotiations during the season," and you had a stalemate with no exit. Porter was entering the final year of his rookie deal. He hadn't played in any of the first three games. And when the Steelers shopped him, Dallas made the only serious offer, even though Detroit had shown interest.

So here's my honest take on piece number one. I understand the philosophy. Second-year guarantees are how teams get stuck paying players who can't stay on the field, and Porter came into this season with a back issue. That's not a crazy concern. But the philosophy also has a cost, and we're seeing it right now. When you won't guarantee the money, you lose young players at premium positions, and then you have to go replace them in the middle of a season. The question isn't whether the Steelers' philosophy is defensible. It is. The question is whether they had a replacement plan ready when they pulled the trigger. And the answer so far looks like no.

Piece number two: Sean Murphy-Bunting.

On Monday, the Steelers signed Murphy-Bunting off Tampa Bay's practice squad and put him on the 53-man roster. On Tuesday's updated depth chart for the Colts game, he's listed as the top backup at cornerback, with Asante Samuel Jr. as the lone listed starter and Daylen Everette, Jalen Ramsey and Brandin Echols in the mix.

Let's talk about who he actually is. He's 29 years old. He was a second-round pick by the Buccaneers in 2019, and he was part of Tampa's Super Bowl team in the 2020 season. He's got real size and length, which is the profile Pittsburgh clearly likes outside. That's the good part.

Here's the realistic part. Murphy-Bunting hasn't played a regular-season snap since 2024. He spent all of 2025 on Arizona's non-football injury list, then got released and landed back on Tampa's practice squad. So he is not a one-for-one replacement for Porter, and nobody should treat him like one. He's experienced depth. He's a professional who's been through big games. And for a team that just lost two outside corners in a week, that has value. But it's a patch, not a plan.

And yes, Pittsburgh could have gone another direction. Trevon Diggs is still sitting on Seattle's practice squad, with five years of history with Mike McCarthy and three with secondary coach Joe Whitt Jr. The Steelers chose Murphy-Bunting first. That tells me they wanted a cheap, low-commitment body, not another big swing at the position. Whether that's discipline or hesitation depends on how the next two weeks go.

Now, one more update on the secondary, because it matters for Sunday. McCarthy said Monday that Jamel Dean is "making progress" with his ankle and was scheduled to be evaluated Wednesday morning, but the expectation is still that he misses multiple weeks. And DeShon Elliott, who's eligible to come off injured reserve, got a strong review from his head coach: "DeShon's making progress. He's chomping at the bit and he's definitely making progress. I don't have a timeline, but I thought he had a really good workout last Wednesday." There's an open roster spot on the 53. Steelers Depot noted it could end up being Elliott's. That would be the most important "addition" of the week, and it wouldn't cost a thing.

Piece number three: the hole in the middle of the defensive line.

While everybody was focused on the corners, the Steelers lost something up front that might matter just as much. Derrick Harmon, the guy who made the game-winning strip-sack against Cincinnati, went on injured reserve Monday with a foot injury. The good news is that it isn't expected to be season-ending. The bad news is he's out at least four games, and probably several weeks.

So look at how the depth chart changed. Keeanu Benton moved up to a starting defensive end spot. Yahya Black is now listed as the starting nose tackle. Sebastian Joseph-Day is expected to rotate in more next to Cameron Heyward. That's a lot of change in the trenches in one week.

And here's why it matters more than it looks on paper. Replacing a defensive lineman isn't just about putting a body in his spot. It's about gap discipline. It's about holding up against double teams so your linebackers can run free. In Cleveland, Pittsburgh's interior players graded poorly against the run, and the Browns rushed for 112 yards. Black stepped into a bigger role after Harmon went down and made five tackles, but run-defense grades from Pro Football Focus for Black, Joseph-Day and Benton were all rough that night.

And when the line gets moved, the linebackers get exposed. Patrick Queen and Payton Wilson both missed too many tackles in Cleveland. Wilson led the team with nine tackles and had a sack, but there were some ugly misses in the run game. If the interior gets pushed around on early downs, offenses stay ahead of the chains, and the pass rush never gets the third-and-long situations it lives on. That's the hidden cost of the Harmon injury. You don't just lose a player. You lose the downs where T.J. Watt gets to tee off.

Piece number four: Elandon Roberts.

This is an interesting one, and honestly, I think it deserves more attention. Roberts is a veteran linebacker the Steelers know very well. He played for Pittsburgh in 2023 and 2024, went to Las Vegas in 2025 where he made 90 tackles, and here's the key part: his defensive coordinator in Las Vegas was Patrick Graham, the same man running Pittsburgh's defense now. The Steelers brought Roberts back late in training camp this summer on a one-year deal, he played well in the preseason, with 10 tackles across 73 snaps in the second and third preseason games, and then he was surprisingly released at final cuts on August 30th.

And right after that, Omar Khan said this: "Not closing the door on that by any means. I have a lot of respect for E-Rob." As of the last reports I could find, Roberts hasn't signed anywhere else.

So does it make sense now? Here's my opinion, and this is opinion, not a report. I think there's a real case. Roberts is 32, he's not going to cover running backs down the sideline, but that's not his job. His job is to fill a gap on first and second down, take on a guard, and hit somebody. For a defense that just lost a starting interior lineman and missed a pile of tackles in Cleveland, a downhill, early-down thumper who already knows Graham's system is exactly the type of cheap insurance that makes sense.

And there's one more angle here that I haven't heard anyone mention. Cole Holcomb, another linebacker on this roster, was fined last week for a blindside block on a kickoff. Under the NFL's new 2026 system, three violations in the major health and safety categories in one season can lead to a suspension, and Holcomb now has one. That doesn't mean he's getting suspended. But it means linebacker depth has a little less margin than it did a month ago. A veteran like Roberts would protect you against that, too.

Piece number five: Kamari Lassiter, and this is the big one.

Let's go back to that Texans game I mentioned at the top. Houston is 0-4. And in that loss to Dallas, Lassiter allowed eight catches for 136 yards on 11 targets, including a 53-yard contested catch to, of all people, former Steeler George Pickens. He was flagged three times, one of those holding calls led to a Cowboys touchdown, and the league fined him 13,659 dollars for an unnecessary roughness hit. It was the most yards any cornerback has allowed in a single game this season. After the game, Lassiter said, "That's probably one of the only glimpses of hope is that every game that we've lost we could have won."

Now, don't let one bad afternoon fool you. Lassiter is a legitimate young corner. He was a second-round pick, 42nd overall in 2024, out of Georgia, the same program that produced Pittsburgh rookie Daylen Everette. He's only 23 years old. Through his first two seasons, he had seven interceptions and 27 passes defensed. ESPN's Bill Barnwell has called him worthy of a first-round pick in a hypothetical trade. Sports Illustrated's Jacob Punturi listed him among five trade targets for Pittsburgh's secondary, calling him the "unsung hero of the Texans defense."

And the fit makes sense on the field. Porter was a press-man corner who wanted to fight receivers at the line all day. Lassiter is more of a smart, instinctive player who can work in off coverage and zone-match concepts, the kind of quarters and match looks a coordinator like Patrick Graham likes to mix in. So if Pittsburgh went after him, it wouldn't be replacing Porter's style. It would be choosing a different style.

But here's where I have to push back on some of the chatter, and I want to be really clear about it. I've seen people suggest Pittsburgh could get Lassiter for roughly the same price it got for Porter, a 2028 second and a 2027 sixth. I don't buy that at all. If Barnwell is in the first-round neighborhood, a second and a sixth isn't going to get Houston to pick up the phone, even at 0-4.

And here's the part that makes this whole idea kind of ironic. Lassiter is under contract through next season, and he becomes extension-eligible in 2027. Some analysts have projected that his next deal could approach the kind of money top corners get, in the range of 30 million a year. So think about what that means. Pittsburgh just traded Porter because it wouldn't guarantee second-year money to a young corner asking for around 28 million a year. If they trade for Lassiter, they'd be paying premium draft capital for a young corner who's going to ask for the exact same kind of contract next offseason. Unless the Steelers' philosophy changes, you'd be running straight back into the same wall.

So my opinion on Lassiter is this, and again, opinion, not a report. He's a smart idea only if Pittsburgh is willing to admit that its contract philosophy needs a carve-out for young corners. If it isn't, then trading a premium pick for him is just buying the Porter problem again, a year later, at a higher price. The trade deadline is November 10th. If Houston is still winless in a few weeks and decides to sell, the Steelers should at least make the call. But they'd better know what they're going to pay him before they hang up.

And to be fair, Lassiter isn't the only name on that Sports Illustrated list. Punturi also floated Arizona's Denzel Burke, a second-year corner who had three interceptions and 11 pass breakups as a rookie; the Chargers' Cam Hart, a 6-foot-2 corner still on his rookie deal; Tennessee's Cor'Dale Flott, on a contract averaging about 15 million a year; and Tampa Bay's Zyon McCollum, on a three-year, 48 million dollar deal. Notice the pattern. The cheap options are young players on rookie contracts, which means the extension question comes right back around. The veteran options come with salaries Pittsburgh would have to absorb mid-season. There's no free lunch at cornerback. There never is.

That's also why Sunday's result matters so much for the deadline. Beat the Colts and go to 3-2, and the front office can afford to be patient, wait for Dean, wait for Elliott, and shop selectively. Lose and fall to 2-3, with a road trip to Tampa next, and the pressure to make a bigger move gets very loud, very fast. Front offices say they don't make decisions based on one game. But one game can absolutely change how much they're willing to pay.

Quickly, on the other name on the trade radar. Alex Highsmith has been on The Athletic's watch list ahead of the deadline, and some projections suggest he could bring back a second-round pick. But with Harmon on injured reserve and the interior already shuffled, I don't see how you subtract a starting pass rusher right now. If Pittsburgh is still struggling into the offseason, that conversation changes. In October, in Aaron Rodgers' final season, you keep him.

So let's put the five pieces on the table and answer the question. Plan, or reaction?

Here's my scorecard. The Porter trade: that was a plan, whether you like it or not. It came straight out of a long-standing contract philosophy, and they got real draft capital for a player who wasn't going to play for them on their terms. Murphy-Bunting: reaction. Smart, cheap, sensible, but a reaction to an injury and a trade that left the cupboard bare. The defensive line after Harmon: reaction, but a reasonable one, using the depth they already had. Roberts: if they make that call, it's a reaction, but a cheap one that fits the coordinator. And Lassiter: that would be a plan, but only if it comes with a new answer to the contract question.

So the honest verdict is: it's mostly reaction right now. That's not a disaster. Every team reacts to injuries in October. But the Steelers made a deliberate choice to move Porter, and deliberate choices should come with a backup plan that's better than a veteran who didn't play last season and a rookie making his first start.

Now let's bring it back to Sunday, because Indianapolis is coming to Acrisure Stadium on October 11th, and this is where all of these pieces get tested at once.

Watch the cornerbacks first. Samuel will start. The question is whether Everette or Murphy-Bunting gets the other outside spot, and how quickly the Colts go after whoever it is. Then watch the safeties. On the depth chart, Robert Spears-Jennings is still listed behind Rayshawn Jenkins, even after Spears-Jennings came in for him in Cleveland and made seven tackles with two pass breakups and an interception. Steelers Depot said that order could flip soon. I think it should flip right now. And watch whether Elliott gets activated, because that would change the entire back end.

Up front, the test is early downs. If Benton, Black and Joseph-Day can hold the line without Harmon, Queen and Wilson can play fast, and T.J. Watt gets the third-and-long situations he feasts on. And it's his birthday on Sunday, by the way. If the interior gets pushed around, you'll see a lot of second-and-four, and that's when this defense gets uncomfortable.

The Steelers are 2-2, one game behind Cleveland and Baltimore. Nothing is lost. But the moves Pittsburgh makes over the next five weeks, before November 10th, are going to decide whether this season looks like a plan or a scramble. Murphy-Bunting was move number one. I don't think it'll be the last.
