Steelers Nation, let me put your mind at ease right away, because I know how a headline like this lands. Nobody is benching Jaylen Warren. Nobody is cutting Rico Dowdle. Mike McCarthy hasn’t announced a new depth chart, and there’s no injury forcing a shake-up in the backfield. If you clicked in a panic thinking the best running back on this team just lost his job, breathe. He didn’t.

So what’s the “change” people are talking about? It’s subtler than a demotion, and honestly, it’s more interesting. It’s about how Pittsburgh splits the handoffs between two backs who are both playing well, and, even more importantly, what this offense chooses to do when it gets close to the end zone. Because if you go back and watch the Browns game, the 27-6 win that sent the Steelers into the bye, the running game was dominant between the twenties and strangely invisible at the goal line.

That’s what we’re breaking down today. The numbers behind Warren and Dowdle. Why their workloads were closer than you’d think against Cleveland. The red-zone sequences that left points on the field. The tight ends who’ve quietly become the best scoring weapons on the roster. How all of it ties into the receiver market on deadline day. And of course, my board at the end, opinion, not a report. The deadline is today, Tuesday, November 10, at 4 p.m. Eastern.

THE STARTING POINT: THIS WAS SUPPOSED TO BE A PASSING TEAM

Let’s set the table with a little history.

When McCarthy took over, a lot of people assumed this would be a pass-heavy offense. That’s been his reputation for years, going back to Green Bay and Dallas. The transcript this video is based on points to his last Cowboys team in 2024, saying Dallas threw 637 passes that season, with Cooper Rush accounting for 308 of them. I couldn’t independently confirm those exact totals, so treat them as reported. But the general idea is right: McCarthy’s offenses have historically lived through the air.

The transcript also says Pittsburgh has thrown 290 passes through eight games, which works out to a little over thirty-six attempts a game. Again, I’d treat that number as reported. But if it’s close, that’s not a team abandoning the pass. It’s a team that has found a second identity on the ground and keeps leaning on it whenever it works.

And here’s the twist. Even with a future Hall of Fame quarterback, the Steelers’ most reliable weapon this season hasn’t been the passing game. It’s been the guy taking the handoffs.

JAYLEN WARREN: THE FEATURED PLAYER

Let’s start with Warren, because his numbers are the reason none of this is a real threat to his job.

He goes into the second half of the season with 704 rushing yards on 119 carries, plus 294 receiving yards. That’s 998 yards from scrimmage, second in the NFL, according to Steelers.com. Two more yards anywhere on the field, and he would have hit a thousand in eight games.

Against Cleveland, he ran 17 times for 143 yards and a touchdown, tying his career high. One correction to the transcript here: it says 133 yards. The team’s official recap says 143.

Back in October, McCarthy put it plainly: “Jaylen’s our featured player right now.” Nothing since then has changed that. If anything, Warren has only strengthened his case. He’s averaging just under six yards a carry for the season. He’s been a weapon in the passing game. And according to Steelers.com, he’s been one of the best backs in the league at picking up the blitz.

So let’s be clear. Any “change at running back” is not about replacing Warren. It’s about what happens around him.

RICO DOWDLE: THE BACK WHO KEEPS COMING BACK

Now let’s talk about the other half of the backfield, because this is where the conversation gets interesting.

Rico Dowdle came to Pittsburgh to be a complementary piece, and he’s carved out a real role. Against Cleveland, according to DraftSharks, he carried the ball 15 times for 56 yards and a touchdown, a 9-yard run in the first quarter that opened the scoring. Warren had 17 carries. That’s a gap of just two carries.

If you’d asked most fans after that game to guess the split, I think they’d have guessed something more lopsided. Warren had the long runs, the highlight plays, the big yards. But Dowdle kept rotating in, series after series.

Look at the snap counts. DraftSharks has Warren at 34 snaps and Dowdle at 28 against Cleveland. That’s close to an even rotation for a back averaging more than eight yards a carry in that game against one averaging under four.

Then go back a week, to the 16-10 win over the Saints in Paris. According to The Fantasy Footballers, Dowdle had nine carries for 41 yards, plus six catches on six targets for 45 more. That was 86 scrimmage yards, a season high, on 15 touches, also a season high. He actually led the team in receptions that day. Warren was still the lead back, with 18 carries for 101 yards and three catches for 36, but Dowdle showed he could be a real weapon in the passing game.

That Paris performance is why I think Dowdle deserves more than token snaps. When he’s on the field, Rodgers has a reliable outlet. If a deep shot isn’t there, Dowdle is the checkdown that turns a dead play into a four-yard gain and keeps the offense on schedule.

HOW I’D SPLIT THE WORK

So what’s the right rotation? Here’s how I’d approach it, and I want to be specific.

First, Warren gets the opening series. Every time. He’s the most explosive player on this offense, and I want the defense feeling him early. Make the linebackers and safeties deal with his cuts and his contact balance right from the first snap.

Second, when Dowdle comes in, give him a full series, not a single carry. There’s a big difference between sending a back in for one handoff and then waving him back to the sideline, and actually letting him run a possession. If Dowdle gains four on first down and then catches a pass for a first down, keep him in. Let him build rhythm. That’s the best way to see what he really gives you.

Third, stay flexible within the series. If Warren breaks a long run and taps his helmet for a breather, send in Dowdle without hesitation. And if Warren comes back fresh two plays later and it’s third-and-short, put the ball in his hands.

That’s the beauty of having two backs who can both play. You don’t have to lock yourself into a rigid plan. You can make the decision snap by snap.

THE REAL ISSUE: WHAT HAPPENED AT THE GOAL LINE

Now we get to the part that matters most, and it’s not about who gets more carries between the twenties. It’s about what Pittsburgh did when it had a chance to put the ball in the end zone.

Here’s the sequence the transcript describes against Cleveland, and it’s worth walking through slowly. The Steelers are up seven. First-and-goal at the two-yard line. Rodgers throws a fade to DK Metcalf. Incomplete. On second down, Rodgers has to throw the ball away. On third down, Warren finally gets the handoff, and he gets stopped. Field goal.

Then, on the next possession, Pittsburgh gets down to the eight. Another throwaway. An incomplete pass intended for tight end Darnell Washington. A sack. Field goal again.

I should note that I couldn’t confirm every snap of those sequences in the official recap, so take the play-by-play as the transcript describes it. But the shape of the first half lines up with the scoring: the Steelers went into halftime up 20-6 after a Dowdle touchdown, two field goals and a late touchdown right before the break.

Two trips inside the ten in the same half, and both ended in field goals. That’s the conversation I want to have.

WHY THE FADE ON FIRST-AND-GOAL BOTHERS ME

Let me be fair to McCarthy and Rodgers. I understand the appeal of the fade to Metcalf. He’s six-foot-four. He’s built for jump balls. On paper, a one-on-one in the corner should favor him.

But here’s what bugs me. That same afternoon, the Steelers were gashing Cleveland on the ground. The transcript says Pittsburgh ran for 160 yards before halftime. I couldn’t confirm that exact first-half number, but Warren’s 143 for the game and the team’s dominance up front suggest the ground game was rolling.

So picture being one of those offensive linemen. You’ve just driven a defense down the field. You’re two yards from a touchdown. And the first call is a pass. Then another pass. Then, finally, a handoff on third down, when the defense knows it’s coming.

Here’s how I’d think about it. If Warren gets stopped on first down, fine. Now you’ve seen how Cleveland is defending the run at the goal line, and Rodgers still has two downs to work with. If you wait until third down to run it, you get exactly one shot on the ground, and if it fails, the possession is over.

I don’t mind a defense winning a snap. That happens. I just want our two backs to get a real say in how we play those three downs.

THE CALL I LOVED: FREIERMUTH’S SNEAK

Now, in fairness, there was a goal-line call that I absolutely loved.

Right before halftime, McCarthy passed on a short field goal and went for it. Tight end Pat Freiermuth took it in himself on a one-yard sneak with one second left, giving Pittsburgh a 20-6 halftime lead. That’s from Steelers.com’s recap.

Think about that from Cleveland’s perspective. You have to account for Warren and Dowdle. You have to respect Rodgers throwing. And now the tight end is taking the ball across the goal line himself. That’s the kind of creativity I want to see more of inside the five.

And it’s a reminder that “change at running back” doesn’t have to mean more carries for one guy. It can mean more variety. More ways to punch it in.

THE TIGHT ENDS: PITTSBURGH’S SECRET SCORING WEAPON

Speaking of tight ends, this is the stat in the transcript that jumped out at me.

According to the transcript, Freiermuth has caught four of Rodgers’ touchdown passes, and Washington has caught three. That would be seven of the thirteen touchdown passes Rodgers threw before the bye. I couldn’t confirm those splits independently, so treat them as reported, but they line up with what we’ve seen on the field.

And Washington’s day against Cleveland was impressive. According to Steelers.com, he had three catches for 94 yards, a career high, including a 58-yard catch-and-run that set up Warren’s third-quarter touchdown.

Here’s why that matters for the goal line. If Cincinnati crowds the line of scrimmage expecting Warren, you’ve got a linebacker who has to decide: step downhill to stop the run, or stay with a big tight end sneaking out behind him. That’s a nightmare for a defense.

So when I say I want something different near the end zone, I’m not just asking for more handoffs. I’m asking for the full menu. Warren up the middle. Dowdle on a quick pitch. Play-action to Washington or Freiermuth. And yes, the occasional tight-end sneak. Make the defense guess.

THE METCALF QUESTION AT THE GOAL LINE

Now let’s talk about Metcalf, because he’s at the center of that fade conversation.

His Sundays have been hard to predict. The transcript credits him with a 51-yard catch in Paris and 56 yards in that game. I couldn’t confirm that play in the recaps I checked, so let’s call it reported. Against Cleveland, he had one catch for four yards on six targets, according to Steelers.com’s Asked and Answered.

The transcript also says Metcalf still leads the team in receiving yards and that rookie Germie Bernard is right behind him in catches, 32 to 33. I couldn’t confirm those exact totals, but Bernard has clearly earned a real role. One fix: the transcript calls him “Jeremy Bernard.” It’s Germie, the second-round pick out of Alabama.

Here’s how I’d use Metcalf. Get him involved early with something he can catch on the move. A slant. A crosser. Let him run through a tackle and build some confidence. Then, once he’s in rhythm, take the shot in the end zone. Don’t make the fade the first thing you ask him to do on a day when the ground game is rolling.

WHY THE ROTATION ALSO PROTECTS WARREN FOR JANUARY

There’s another reason I like giving Dowdle real series, and it has nothing to do with this week.

Warren is on pace for a huge season, and he’s doing it with a running style that invites contact. He breaks arm tackles, he bounces off linebackers, and he finishes runs. That’s what makes him great. It’s also what wears a back down over seventeen games.

If the goal is January, and in Pittsburgh it always is, then the smartest thing McCarthy can do is keep Warren fresh for the games that matter most. Every series Dowdle handles in November is a series Warren’s legs don’t have to absorb. Every third-quarter possession in a comfortable win is a chance to save a few hits.

That’s not a knock on Warren. It’s an investment in him. Teams that win in the playoffs usually have a lead back who still has burst in late December. The rotation is how you make sure that happens.

THE GOAL-LINE MENU I’D WANT TO SEE

Let me get specific about what I’d want on the call sheet inside the five-yard line, because “run it more” isn’t a plan by itself.

Option one: Warren on an inside zone, behind Zach Frazier and a line that has been creating movement all season. That’s the base play, and it should be the first call more often than not.

Option two: Dowdle on a quick outside run, attacking the edge if the defense packs the middle. Different back, different look.

Option three: play-action with a tight end leaking out. If the linebackers bite on the run fake, Washington or Freiermuth should be open in the flat or the back of the end zone.

Option four: the tight-end sneak. We’ve already seen it work. Keep it in the pocket for the right moment.

And option five: the fade to Metcalf. I’m not saying never. I’m saying not first. Use it once the defense has been forced to respect everything else.

Five different looks, three different ball carriers and two different tight ends. That’s how you turn a dominant run game into touchdowns instead of field goals.

DEADLINE DAY: WHAT THIS MEANS FOR THE RECEIVER HUNT

All of this connects directly to today’s trade deadline.

The latest on Josh Downs: on Sunday, according to Heavy, ESPN’s Adam Schefter reported that the Colts are expected to trade Downs before the deadline for a fourth-round pick, and that “Teams looking for a receiver are interested.” The Steelers have been linked all along, along with the Chiefs and Commanders.

Remember, Pittsburgh doesn’t have its own 2027 fourth-round pick. That went to Dallas in the Broderick Jones trade. So the Steelers would need to use a 2028 pick, a different round or some kind of creative swap to match the price.

But there’s a new wrinkle worth mentioning. Gerry Dulac of the Post-Gazette raised a fit question, according to Steeler Nation. “At 5 feet 9 and 171 pounds,” Dulac wrote, “Downs does not appear to be the type of receiver Mike McCarthy appears to favor.” He added: “The Steelers coach prefers big receivers who are built to play in the cold-weather climates in the northeast.”

That’s a fair point, and it ties right into today’s topic. McCarthy likes size. Metcalf is big. Washington is big. Freiermuth is big. If McCarthy wants big bodies near the goal line, a smaller slot receiver may not be his top priority.

Dulac’s broader read, according to the transcript, is that a depth addition is more likely than an expensive deal. ESPN’s Jeremy Fowler has named Downs and Brian Thomas Jr. as two receivers to watch league-wide. And there’s a real argument for patience too. Michael Pittman Jr. and Roman Wilson are both on injured reserve, and the Steelers expect to get them back. Bernard already knows the system and has Rodgers’ trust.

So when I think about whether a receiver trade would change the goal-line problem, the honest answer is: probably not by itself. The fix near the end zone is about play-calling, not personnel.

WHAT ABOUT GARRETT WILSON?

The transcript mentions Garrett Wilson as a dream option, and I get it. He’s a talented receiver. But according to NBC Sports’ trade tracker, the Jets reportedly aren’t open to trading him, and Wilson has said he wants to stay. If you’re trying to figure out what actually happens on Sunday, that’s not a realistic path.

THE FANTASY ANGLE

For those of you who play fantasy football, here’s a quick take.

Warren is a weekly starter. No question. Second in the league in scrimmage yards, featured role, and a coach who calls him the featured player.

Dowdle is more interesting than his depth-chart spot suggests. He’s had a game where catches did most of the work, like Paris, and a game where he got fifteen carries, like Cleveland. Watch his first series in Cincinnati. If he’s getting a full possession and staying in on passing downs when the Steelers are trailing, that tells you more about his weekly value than a few late carries with a big lead.

And for Rodgers owners, remember: the Freiermuth sneak is a reminder that the Steelers can score without the touchdown showing up next to your quarterback’s name. Pittsburgh can get better in the red zone without Rodgers’ passing numbers changing at all.

THE CINCINNATI TEST

So why does all of this matter so much right now? Because the next game is the first real test of whether the Steelers learned from the Browns game.

On Sunday, November 15, Pittsburgh visits Cincinnati. The Bengals are the closest team chasing the Steelers in the AFC North. Pittsburgh won the first meeting in Week 3. But Cincinnati has an offense that can score in bunches, with Joe Burrow coming off a game against Tennessee before the Steelers’ bye in which he was involved in six touchdowns and threw for more than four hundred seventy yards.

Against a team like that, field goals won’t be enough. Every red-zone trip has to count.

Here’s what I’ll be watching.

First, the opening series. Warren should get the first carry. Let Cincinnati feel him.

Second, Dowdle’s role. If he gets a full series with a lead, fine. If he gets one in a tight game when Pittsburgh needs a first down to keep Burrow on the sideline, that’s a real vote of confidence.

Third, the first trip inside the five. What’s the first call? If it’s a handoff or a play-action to a tight end, that tells me the staff took the Browns lesson seriously. If it’s another fade, I’ll be nervous.

And if Dowdle gets the Steelers to the two-yard line on his series, I’d leave him in for the next snap. Don’t check a carry count while the offense is ready to finish the drive. Let the guy who got you there have a chance to score.

MY TRADE BLOCK BOARD (OPINION, NOT A REPORT)

It’s deadline day, so here’s my board. Remember: this is opinion, not a report.

Josh Downs: yes for a 2028 fourth-round pick. I hear Dulac’s point about size, and it’s a fair one. But a reliable slot receiver doesn’t need to win jump balls. He needs to win third downs. If the Colts demand a third with nothing coming back, I walk.

Brian Thomas Jr.: pass at a Day 2 price. Too expensive with a quarterback decision coming after the season.

Running back: no move. Warren and Dowdle are enough. The fix is usage, not personnel.

Tight ends: already a strength. Keep using them near the goal line.

The core: untouchable. Watt, Highsmith, Herbig, Queen, Ramsey, Warren, Frazier.

FINAL WORD

Here’s my bottom line. The “surprising change at running back” isn’t about replacing anyone. Jaylen Warren is the featured player, and he’s earned it. The real change I want to see is in how the Steelers use both of their backs, and what they do once they get close to the end zone.

Against Cleveland, the run game dominated between the twenties and then disappeared at the goal line. Against Cincinnati, that can’t happen. Hand it off on first down. Use the tight ends. Keep a sneak in the pocket. And trust the two backs who got you there.
