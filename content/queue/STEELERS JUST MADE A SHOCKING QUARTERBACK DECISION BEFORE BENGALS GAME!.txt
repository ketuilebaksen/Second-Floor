THE QUARTERBACK DECISION THAT ACTUALLY HAPPENED

Let’s go back to the end of August.

Everyone expected the Steelers to keep three quarterbacks: Aaron Rodgers, a veteran backup and one of the two young guys. Instead, they kept all four. According to Steelers Wire, published on Yahoo Sports on August 30, Pittsburgh’s initial 53-man roster included Rodgers, Mason Rudolph, Will Howard and rookie Drew Allar.

That’s rare. The same piece described four-quarterback rooms on a 53-man roster as unusual. Most teams can’t afford to spend that many spots on one position.

The reasoning came out a week earlier. According to Pro Football Talk, citing Gerry Dulac of the Post-Gazette on August 21, the Steelers had decided to keep both Howard and Allar because neither was likely to clear waivers and make it back to the practice squad. In plain English: if they cut either young quarterback, another team would probably grab him.

And Rudolph stayed because, as PFT pointed out, he was the only experienced backup behind a 42-year-old starter. Going into a season without a veteran safety net would have been a big risk.

THE COST OF FOUR QUARTERBACKS

Here’s the part fans don’t think about. Every roster spot spent on a quarterback is one you don’t have somewhere else.

Steelers Wire noted that the four-quarterback decision came alongside the releases of linebacker Elandon Roberts and defensive lineman Dean Lowry. That’s the trade-off: you protect your future at quarterback, and you give up a little depth at other positions.

Now fast-forward to this weekend. Derrick Harmon is questionable to come off injured reserve. According to Yahoo Sports, Pittsburgh has no open spot on the 53-man roster, so activating him requires a corresponding move.

I want to be very careful here. Nobody has reported that a quarterback will be moved to make room for Harmon. Not one source. The corresponding move could be at any position, and I’m not going to speculate about who it might be. But it’s a good reminder of why roster spots matter, and why that August decision still echoes in November.

WHY THE QUARTERBACK FUTURE IS STILL THE BIG STORY

The reason the Steelers paid that price is simple. Rodgers said back in May that this is his final season. So the question isn’t who plays quarterback Sunday night. It’s who plays quarterback in 2027.

Allar was a third-round pick out of Penn State. Howard was a 2025 sixth-rounder out of Ohio State. Keeping both gives Pittsburgh a full season to evaluate them in practice, without losing either one on waivers.

That’s the real quarterback decision. It wasn’t shocking this week. It was bold in August. And it may shape this franchise for years.

THE GAME-DAY QUARTERBACK SETUP

There’s one more wrinkle to the four-quarterback room that’s worth explaining, because it affects Sunday night.

Carrying four quarterbacks on the 53-man roster doesn’t mean all four are active on game day. Teams set a limited game-day roster and declare the rest inactive. As the rules are generally understood, a team can also designate a third quarterback as an emergency option without using one of its regular game-day spots, as long as he’s on the 53-man roster.

So in practice, Pittsburgh has flexibility. Rodgers starts. Rudolph backs him up. And the young quarterbacks spend most weeks developing in practice, with one of them potentially available as an emergency option. That’s a luxury most teams don’t have, and it’s part of why the Steelers were willing to pay for it in roster depth.

It also means the four-quarterback decision is more about the future than about any single Sunday. Nobody expects Howard or Allar to take a meaningful snap against Cincinnati unless something goes very wrong.

RODGERS’ DECISIONS ON THE FIELD

Since we’re talking about quarterback decisions, let’s talk about the ones Rodgers makes on Sundays, because those have been pretty good.

Against Cleveland, according to the AP, Rodgers pushed McCarthy to go for a touchdown from the 1-yard line at the end of the first half instead of kicking a field goal. They went for it, and it worked.

Against Indianapolis in Week 5, Rodgers threw an 80-yard touchdown to Warren. According to TSN, it was his ninth touchdown pass of 80 yards or more, tying Ben Roethlisberger for the NFL record. Rodgers took a big hit from Grover Stewart delivering the ball, and afterward he said: “I feel like I kind of earned it a bit.”

And the most important decision he keeps making? Getting the ball to Warren. Which brings us to Cincinnati.

CREDIT WHERE IT’S DUE: THE PANEL RESPECTS PITTSBURGH

Before we get into the trash talk, I want to point out something the transcript makes clear: this Bengals panel respects the Steelers.

More than once, they said it directly: the Steelers are a good football team. Every game against Pittsburgh is a tough game. One panelist made a smart argument about strength of schedule, too. He said people love to discredit a team’s wins by pointing to the opponents, but division rivals play largely the same schedule. In his view, you can’t dismiss a 6-2 record just because of who was on it.

And they praised this defense specifically. They called it a real strength and named Watt, Highsmith, Herbig and Heyward as the players Cincinnati has to neutralize.

So this isn’t a group of fans dismissing the Steelers. It’s a group of fans who are nervous about them. And honestly, that’s the best kind of preview to listen to, because they’re telling you exactly what they fear.

WHAT THE BENGALS PANEL SAID

Let me set the scene. The transcript comes from a group of Bengals-side voices previewing the game, and it got heated fast.

One panelist’s key to the game was crystal clear: if Cincinnati stops Warren from getting four to seven yards per carry, the game’s over. And if it turns into a shootout, he said, Pittsburgh can’t keep up.

Another panelist pushed back immediately. It’s not going to be a shootout, he said, because the Steelers will keep the ball away from you.

I’m with the second guy. And the transcript’s own narrator agrees. As he put it: get me second-and-four after a Warren carry, and suddenly McCarthy can do anything. Run again. Play action. Take a shot if the safety creeps up. String three or four of those snaps together, and Joe Burrow is standing on the sideline watching the clock.

That’s the whole game plan in one sentence.

JAYLEN WARREN BY THE NUMBERS

So let’s talk about the man Cincinnati has circled.

Through eight games, according to Yardbarker, Warren had 704 rushing yards, sixth in the NFL, plus 294 receiving yards. The transcript adds that he did it on 119 carries. That’s nearly six yards per carry, and it puts him two yards short of 1,000 yards from scrimmage.

The transcript also says former running back Raheem Mostert put Warren fifth on his list of the best backs in football. I couldn’t find that ranking myself, so take it as the transcript’s report. But with those numbers, a top-five ranking doesn’t sound crazy.

Then there’s the Colts game on October 11, the one the transcript tells you to go watch. According to TSN, Warren had 188 total yards and two long touchdowns: the 80-yard catch-and-run in the first quarter and a 50-yard sprint up the middle in the third. He finished the second one by hopping an imaginary hurdle at the goal line. And here’s the stat that blew me away: he became the first player since Herschel Walker in 1986 with a rushing touchdown of at least 50 yards and a receiving touchdown of at least 80 yards in the same game.

Even Rodgers got a laugh out of how little national attention Warren was getting. According to ClutchPoints, when a reporter asked whether Warren was finally getting recognition, Rodgers answered: “Who?” Then he added: “Yeah, but I mean, they have their favorites, and people they want to talk about, so here’s what it is.”

WHAT WARREN DID TO CINCINNATI IN SEPTEMBER

And the Bengals know exactly how dangerous he is, because they lived it.

In the Week 3 game, according to ClutchPoints, Warren carried 17 times for 127 yards and caught three passes for 49 more. That’s 176 yards from scrimmage, a career high at the time. Next Gen Stats credited him with ten missed tackles on 20 touches. And 69 of his rushing yards came before contact, after he’d been at minus-14 before contact through the first two games.

McCarthy called him “a focal point” of the offense. And remember, Warren was listed as questionable with a shoulder injury going into that game.

The panel also brought up a long Warren touchdown run that was called back for holding. One panelist called it 80 yards. The narrator says it was 87. I couldn’t confirm the exact yardage, so I’ll just say this: according to the transcript, Warren had an even bigger number on the board until a flag took it away. That’s how close that game came to being a blowout.

THE THREE PLAYS THAT DECIDED IT

Now, here’s where the transcript’s narrator makes his best point. One Bengals panelist said Pittsburgh doesn’t believe in DK Metcalf. The narrator answered by pointing to what happened late in that game.

According to the transcript, Rodgers went to Metcalf for 14 yards and a first down. Then he found Darnell Washington for 47 yards down the sideline. Then Warren took the next handoff for 26.

Fox19 in Cincinnati confirms the key parts of that. Its breakdown of the Bengals’ mistakes says the defense missed tackles on a 47-yard gain by Washington. And it says a 26-yard run helped set up the winning field goal, with an injured Kyle Dugger still on the field for that play.

Think about what that sequence means. A big receiver moving the chains. A tight end rumbling down the sideline. A running back getting into the secondary. Three different ways to hurt a defense on three straight plays.

DARNELL WASHINGTON: THE MATCHUP CINCINNATI FEARS

That brings us to the biggest admission in the transcript, and it came from a Bengals panelist himself.

He said his team’s secondary is either really good or really bad, with no in-between. And he said the specific problem is covering tight ends: tight ends get loose against Cincinnati.

Now, think about who Rodgers trusts. The panel said it out loud: Darnell Washington is Rodgers’ comfort target. According to the transcript, Washington led Pittsburgh with 67 receiving yards in the first game.

So if the Bengals load up to stop Warren, linebackers start stepping toward the line, and that’s when Rodgers looks for the six-foot-seven tight end running into the space they just left. The transcript’s narrator says that’s exactly what he’d do, and I agree. Make Cincinnati tackle Washington in the open field and see how that goes.

THE BENGALS’ SECONDARY QUESTION

The panel mentioned two safeties who’ll have to play well: Bryan Cook and Jordan Battle. And that’s even more important now.

Kyle Dugger, the former Steeler, has been ruled out with a quad injury. So Cincinnati’s safety depth is thinner than it was in September.

And in September, it was already a problem. According to Fox19, Cook missed most of that game with an ankle injury, and cornerback Jalen Davis missed the second half with a hamstring injury. Fox19 says those absences hurt the Bengals’ tackling. Davis has been practicing again, but he’s listed as doubtful for Sunday.

So the Bengals are hoping their back end shows up on the “really good” side of the panelist’s description. If it shows up on the other side, Warren and Washington are going to have a long night against it.

“THEY DON’T BELIEVE IN DK”

Let’s address the Metcalf comment directly, because it’s fair to ask.

Metcalf has struggled this season. Drops, inconsistent connection with Rodgers, and the deep ball that turned into an interception against Cleveland. That’s real.

But the transcript’s own narrator makes the counterpoint: he’s big, he can run, and he’s due for a breakout game. And in the first meeting with Cincinnati, Metcalf caught a 6-yard touchdown on third-and-2 in the third quarter, his first score of the season, according to the Steelers’ recap.

So, does Pittsburgh believe in Metcalf? The play-calling in the biggest moment of that game says yes. The bigger issue is whether he can turn one or two of those big moments into a full game.

THE BENGALS’ INTERIOR: DEXTER LAWRENCE

Now let’s give Cincinnati its due, because the narrator does too.

The transcript points to defensive tackle Dexter Lawrence as the player who can blow up a run before the back even reaches the line. Lawrence appears on the Bengals’ injury report this week, according to Yahoo Sports, though the report I saw doesn’t specify an injury or give him a game status, so I’d expect him to play.

The narrator’s point is the right one: if Cincinnati can get Pittsburgh into third-and-long, the playbook shrinks. Rodgers stops choosing between run, play action and shots, and starts throwing into a defense that knows what’s coming.

That’s why the early downs matter so much. Win first and second down with Warren and Rico Dowdle, and Lawrence becomes a problem you can manage. Lose them, and he becomes the story of the game.

THE RIGHT TACKLE DEBATE

There was one more great moment in the panel. One Bengals voice bragged about right tackle Amarius Mims, calling that side of the line “Alcatraz” and saying no pressure is coming from there.

Another panelist fired back that Cincinnati had given up five sacks over the last two weeks. I couldn’t verify that number, so treat it as the panel’s claim. But the exchange is a perfect preview of the real battle.

Because here’s what the panel admitted: the key for Cincinnati is neutralizing T.J. Watt, Alex Highsmith, Nick Herbig and Cam Heyward. One panelist even predicted a Watt strip-sack.

And this pass rush has earned that respect. According to the AP, after Week 8, Pittsburgh led the NFL with 23 sacks, and Watt had seven and a half of them, tied for the league lead. The rest came from ten different players. You can lock down one side, but this front attacks from everywhere.

SETTING THE RECORD STRAIGHT ON THE BURROW FUMBLE

One of the panelists described the game-ending play as Burrow holding on to the ball and trying to scramble to the left while Cincinnati was already in field goal range.

Fox19’s breakdown describes it more simply: Burrow took a strip-sack, and the Steelers recovered. That’s how it went down, with Derrick Harmon getting the sack and Sebastian Joseph-Day falling on the ball with 44 seconds left.

The panelist’s broader point is fair, though. Cincinnati was close enough for a game-tying field goal, and a quick throw or a throwaway might have sent the game to overtime. That’s what makes it sting for Bengals fans. Zac Taylor said afterward, according to Fox19: “You feel like you had every opportunity to win it.” And defensive tackle Jonathan Allen said the defense “left too much out there.”

THE HIDDEN STORY: SPECIAL TEAMS

There’s one thing the panel didn’t really talk about, and it might be the biggest lesson from September.

According to Fox19, Cincinnati’s special teams effectively cost it 10 points. A roughing-the-punter penalty kept a Pittsburgh drive alive, and that drive ended in a touchdown. Then a muffed punt led to a Steelers field goal.

In a three-point game, that’s the difference. And it’s a reminder that Sunday night isn’t just about Warren versus Lawrence or Watt versus Mims. It’s about which team avoids the mistakes that hand the other side free points.

THE SHOOTOUT QUESTION

So, let’s go back to the panel’s central argument. Can Pittsburgh keep up in a shootout?

Here’s the honest answer: the Steelers probably don’t want a shootout. Cincinnati is coming off a 34-17 win over Atlanta in Madrid, where Burrow completed 24 of 29 passes for 271 yards and three touchdowns, and Ja’Marr Chase caught ten balls for 155 yards. That’s an offense that can score with anyone.

But that doesn’t mean Pittsburgh can’t win one. In September, the Steelers scored 30 and outgained the Bengals 411 to 352, according to the Steelers’ recap. They converted 7 of 12 third downs. That’s not a team that can’t keep up.

The transcript says McCarthy has shown the formula over recent games: running the ball 30-plus times. I couldn’t confirm the exact carry counts, but the logic holds. The best way to beat a great quarterback is to make sure he doesn’t get the ball. One panelist predicted a 65-35 type of game in terms of run-pass balance. If Pittsburgh’s running backs are getting four to seven yards a carry, that’s exactly the kind of game the Steelers want.

THE ROSTER MATH AFTER SUNDAY

One last note on that quarterback room, because it’ll keep coming up over the next few weeks.

Harmon is the first injured player who may need a roster spot, but he won’t be the last. Roman Wilson becomes eligible to come off injured reserve after this game. Michael Pittman Jr. is working his way back. Sebastian Joseph-Day can return after the Philadelphia trip. Every one of those activations will require a corresponding move.

That’s where carrying four quarterbacks becomes a real puzzle for Omar Khan. I’m not predicting anything, and nothing has been reported. But if the roster gets crowded in December, fans will naturally look at the quarterback room. For now, the Steelers have clearly decided that keeping their young quarterbacks is worth the squeeze.

MY BOARD (OPINION, NOT A REPORT)

Here’s my board for Sunday night. This is opinion, not a report.

Quarterback: no changes. Rodgers starts, and the four-quarterback room stays as is unless the medical staff forces a roster move.

Jaylen Warren: 22 to 25 touches. Mix in screens and swing passes so he gets the ball in space, not just between the tackles.

Rico Dowdle: 10 to 12 carries to keep Warren fresh for the fourth quarter.

Darnell Washington: at least five targets. Attack the tight-end coverage the Bengals’ own panel admitted is a weakness.

DK Metcalf: two easy completions early. Build his confidence before you take the deep shot.

Defense: line Watt and Herbig up over the right tackle and make Mims prove the “Alcatraz” talk. If Harmon is activated, rotate him inside on passing downs.

Special teams: no free points. Clean punts, secure catches, and no roughing penalties.
