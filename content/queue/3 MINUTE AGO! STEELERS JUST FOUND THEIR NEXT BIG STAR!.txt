Steelers Nation, the word “star” gets thrown around way too easily on the internet, so today I want to earn it. No mystery signing happened this morning. Nobody arrived through a trade. What happened is this: across a handful of reports from the Steelers’ own website and some national voices this week, one name keeps rising to the surface as the player who is turning from “nice piece” into “the guy.” And behind him, a couple of young names are starting to make a case of their own.

The player at the center of it is Jaylen Warren. Undrafted. Fifth NFL season. Twenty-seven years old. And right now, sitting second in the entire National Football League in yards from scrimmage.

So let’s do this properly. We’ll walk through the numbers, the quotes and the people around him who are making this possible. Then we’ll look at the young faces on the defensive line who might be the stars after the star. And because the trade deadline is still on the calendar for Tuesday, November 10, at 4 p.m. Eastern, I’ll close with my updated board, labeled clearly as opinion.

THE NUMBERS THAT FORCE THE CONVERSATION

Let me start by fixing a number from the transcript this video is built on, because it matters. The source says Warren has rushed for “74 yards on 119 carries.” That’s a typo, and a big one. According to Teresa Varley’s piece on Steelers.com, published November 4, Warren goes into the bye with 704 rushing yards on 119 carries. That puts him seventh in the NFL in rushing.

Here’s the number that really jumps off the page. Nine hundred ninety-eight yards from scrimmage. That includes 294 receiving yards on top of the rushing total, and it ranks second in the league. Only Jahmyr Gibbs is ahead of him.

Now do a little math with me. Seven hundred four divided by one hundred nineteen. That’s right around five point nine yards every time he touches it on the ground. For a back carrying the load in a real offense, not a change-of-pace guy who gets six handoffs a week, that is absurd.

And it gets wilder. Steeler Nation laid out that Warren is averaging 6.93 yards per touch, leading the NFL by more than a full yard, and that the only single season in league history with a higher number belongs to Marshall Faulk in 1999. I want to be careful with historical claims like that, because the qualifier for “touches” can change the list, so let’s call it what it is: a reported figure that, if it holds, puts Warren’s efficiency next to one of the greatest dual-threat backs who ever played.

Even Steelers.com’s own count, from Brian Batko’s November 3 story, says Warren leads all running backs in yards per touch among players with at least thirty-five combined rushes and receptions. However you slice it, nobody is getting more out of each opportunity than he is.

Against Cleveland in the 27-6 win, he ran for 143 yards. That wasn’t garbage time. That was a back setting the tone in a division game.

WHAT THE PEOPLE AROUND HIM ARE SAYING

Numbers tell you what. Quotes tell you how it feels inside the building. And this week, the quotes were fantastic.

Mike McCarthy described him this way: “He’s hot as a firecracker.” Coming from a head coach who has seen a lot of great backs over his career, that’s not throwaway praise.

DK Metcalf, who knows a thing or two about being hard to tackle, put it even more colorfully: “He runs like a bat out of hell.”

And Zach Frazier, his center, the guy who is leading the blocking up front, said this: “He’s going to get at least 10 yards minimum each play.” Obviously that’s a lineman having fun with it. Nobody averages ten a carry. But it tells you the mindset in that huddle. When the ball goes to Warren, the guys up front expect something big.

So what did Warren himself say about all of it? “I don’t know. I’m just playing ball.”

That’s it. That’s the whole answer. And honestly, that’s exactly the kind of answer you want from a player in the middle of a career year. Back in mid-October, when he was asked about chasing numbers, he told reporters: “The goal is to win, though, at the end of the day.” Same guy, same message, six weeks apart.

THE NATIONAL VOICE THAT TURNED UP THE VOLUME

Here’s where the “star” conversation went beyond Pittsburgh.

On her show, Kay Adams made a declaration that a lot of people outside Western Pennsylvania probably weren’t ready for: “Jaylen Warren is the second-best running back in football right now.” According to Steeler Nation’s write-up, she also argued that the league is underreacting to what he’s doing, and that he makes Pittsburgh more complete than anyone else in the AFC North.

Is that a hot take? A little. But look at the evidence we just walked through. Second in scrimmage yards. Seventh in rushing. Best-in-class efficiency. Huge games in big moments. You can argue with the ranking, but you can’t argue that it’s crazy.

And remember where Warren came from. He wasn’t a first-round pick. He wasn’t a second-round pick. He went undrafted out of Oklahoma State, came to Pittsburgh in 2022, and spent years as the backup who fans kept begging the coaches to use more. Now he’s the engine of a six-and-two team. That’s the definition of a breakout. That’s what “found their next big star” actually means here, not a stranger arriving from somewhere else, but the guy who was here all along finally getting the stage he deserved.

WHY WARREN IS DIFFERENT AS A RUNNER

Let’s talk about what you see on tape, because the stats only tell part of the story.

Warren is not the biggest back in the league. He’s not the tallest. But he runs with a low center of gravity that makes the first defender’s job miserable. Arm tackles slide right off him. When a linebacker comes downhill and doesn’t wrap up, Warren bounces off and keeps his feet moving. That’s how you end up near the top of the league in yards after contact.

Then there’s the burst. When the hole is there, he hits it immediately. He doesn’t dance in the backfield hoping for something better. He trusts the blocking, he reads the second level, and he’s through the line before the safety can fill.

And he’s a weapon in the passing game. Two hundred ninety-four receiving yards is not a gimmick. Rodgers trusts him on checkdowns, on screens, on option routes out of the backfield. When the pass rush gets home fast, Warren is the release valve that turns a potential sack into a five-yard gain, and sometimes a twenty-yard gain.

There’s one more thing people forget. Pass protection. In Varley’s article, Frazier talked about Warren’s blitz pickup as among the best he’s seen. For a forty-two-year-old quarterback who doesn’t run away from pressure like he used to, a back who will stand in the hole and stone a blitzing linebacker is worth his weight in gold.

THE BIG MAN IN THE MIDDLE: ZACH FRAZIER

Now, the transcript makes a big deal about Zach Frazier, and rightfully so. But I want to give you the honest version, because the honest version is actually more interesting.

In the November 3 edition of Asked and Answered on Steelers.com, a fan wrote in to say he believes Frazier “will be an all-time great” and could be top three in the league at his position, maybe higher.

Bob Labriola didn’t fully buy it. Here’s what he did say: Frazier is “a very good player, a team leader, right now the alpha among the team’s offensive linemen.” That’s real praise. The alpha of the offensive line in only his third year.

But then Labriola pumped the brakes. He pointed out that Frazier “hasn’t even been voted to his first Pro Bowl, let alone All-Pro,” and called the all-time-great talk “premature and a little bit unfair to him.” He compared the timeline to Steelers legends at center, noting that Mike Webster didn’t start full-time until his fourth season.

I actually love that answer. It’s the right balance. Frazier is clearly special. He’s the guy calling the shots up front. But “all-time great” is a label you earn over a decade, not over two and a half seasons. Let him be great first, and then we’ll talk about history.

And the coaching staff is clearly on the same page about his importance. In Brian Batko’s November 3 story, offensive coordinator Brian Angelichio said: “Fraz does as phenomenal a job as anyone I’ve ever been around.” The article describes Frazier as an extension of the coaching staff on the field. He sets the identifications. He decides who’s picking up whom. He gets to the second level without losing his balance, which is exactly what you need from a center in a zone-heavy run game.

So when you see Warren hitting a crease at full speed, a lot of the time it’s because Frazier made the right call pre-snap and moved the protection the right way. Stars don’t happen in a vacuum.

THE OFFENSIVE LINE IS BECOMING A REAL UNIT

Let’s zoom out to the whole group up front, because the numbers here are encouraging.

According to that same Steelers.com piece, Pittsburgh ranks third in the NFL with 4.9 yards per carry. The Steelers are tied for seventh with 58 rushing first downs, and they’re in the top ten in both yards before contact and yards after contact.

Think about what that combination means. Yards before contact tells you the line is opening holes. Yards after contact tells you the backs are finishing runs. When you’re top ten in both, the run game is working on every level.

Angelichio talked about how this group is coming together. His words: “they’re starting to get a feel of the concepts.” And on the connection inside the room: “this group is up there with any of them as far as the connectivity.” The line has had six games in its current configuration, and you can see the comfort building. Offensive line coach James Campen gets a lot of credit for that, and Angelichio made a point of saying, “Certainly, Campy’s got input on stuff he likes” in the run-game planning.

There’s an honest flip side, though. The Steelers rank fourteenth in rushing yards per game. That’s not a contradiction. It’s a volume issue. The run game is efficient, but there have been games where the team just didn’t stay with it as much as it could have. That’s something the staff can fix with play-calling, and with Warren playing like this, I’d expect them to lean on him more.

THE PASSING GAME QUESTION

The other thing Angelichio was candid about is the passing game. The Steelers.com story says it may be lagging “to an extent,” and Angelichio pointed to something very specific: “A lot of it is within the time clock of the quarterback’s feet.” In other words, timing. When the quarterback’s drop and the receivers’ breaks are perfectly in sync, the ball comes out on rhythm. When they’re a half-beat off, the whole play stalls.

And Rodgers himself agreed that there’s work to do. After the Browns win, he called it “Not very complete,” and added: “Defense played excellent. Special teams did fine. But offense should have had a lot more points.” That’s a veteran holding his own unit to a higher standard even after a 21-point win.

Angelichio said those are the areas the staff planned to dive into during the bye. And here’s why that’s relevant to Warren: the better the passing game gets, the fewer eight-man boxes he sees. If defenses have to respect Rodgers throwing on time, Warren’s lanes get even bigger. A star running back and a functioning passing game feed each other.

One more note on the passing game. In that same Asked and Answered, Labriola pointed out that Metcalf had a team-high 58 targets entering the Browns game, then had six targets and just one catch for four yards against Cleveland. Labriola’s view is that Metcalf’s struggles aren’t about effort and that the team needs his production later in the season. That’s the most important storyline for the offense coming out of the bye.

THE STARS AFTER THE STAR: THE YOUNG DEFENSIVE LINE

Now let’s flip to defense, because this is where the “next” in “next big star” really applies.

Pittsburgh’s defensive line has been hit hard. Derrick Harmon is on injured reserve. So is Sebastian Joseph-Day. Those are two important bodies inside, and losing both in the same stretch would normally be a crisis.

Instead, two young players have stepped into the gap.

The first is Gabriel Rubio, a sixth-round pick out of Notre Dame in the 2026 draft. He’s six-foot-five, around 320 pounds, and he spent five seasons in South Bend before coming to Pittsburgh. According to the Steelers’ bye week blog, he’s played in each of the last four games. When asked what he’s focused on improving, Rubio said: “Anchoring against the run,” along with generating more pass pressure. That’s a self-aware young player. He knows what the job is.

The second is Kevin Jobity Jr. Let me fix the transcript here. The source calls him “Kevin Joy Jr.” His name is Kevin Jobity Jr., an undrafted rookie out of Syracuse, where he was a four-year contributor. And here’s the headline moment: Jobity recorded the first sack of his NFL career against Cleveland. The transcript also claims he made his debut against the Saints in Paris, but I couldn’t confirm that detail, so I’m leaving it out. What I can confirm is the sack, and that’s the part that matters.

Defensive line coach Domata Peko, another name the transcript garbled, has been clear about how he sees it. “We’ve got young Rubio and Jobity out there now,” he said. And then: “I’m happy with the progression of where we’re going right now.” And finally: “I feel like we’re on the rise.”

Here’s the stat that backs him up. According to Batko’s October 30 story, the Steelers allowed an average of just 62 rushing yards per game over a three-game stretch in which Harmon was absent for all three and Joseph-Day missed the most recent one. Then came the Browns game, when Pittsburgh held Cleveland to 34 rushing yards and piled up eight sacks. That’s not a defense hanging on by a thread. That’s a defense that’s getting stronger even while its depth chart gets thinner.

Are Rubio and Jobity stars? Not yet. Let’s be realistic. A sixth-round pick and an undrafted free agent don’t become Pro Bowlers in eight weeks. But this is how stars start: the injury opens a door, the young guy walks through it, and the coaching staff sees enough to keep him on the field. Jaylen Warren’s career started the same way.

PATRICK QUEEN: THE PLAYER OF THE WEEK

And then there’s the veteran who tied the whole defensive performance together.

Patrick Queen was named the Steelers’ Player of the Week after the Browns game, in Bob Labriola’s column on Steelers.com. Here’s the line: 12 tackles, one tackle for loss, a sack and a half, a pass defensed and a forced fumble that Keeanu Benton recovered. Labriola summed it up by writing that Queen “had his fingerprints all over all of that.”

That’s the kind of game that silences a lot of criticism. Queen has heard plenty of it over his time in Pittsburgh. But when the defensive line is relying on a rookie sixth-rounder and an undrafted free agent, the linebacker behind them has to be the organizer. He has to get everyone lined up, recognize what’s coming and clean up anything that leaks through. Against Cleveland, Queen did all of that.

And the timing matters. The next game, coming out of the bye, is in Cincinnati on November 15. The Bengals are five and two, the closest team chasing the Steelers in the division. Pittsburgh beat them back in Week 3. A linebacker playing at Queen’s level is exactly what you want for a rematch with a team that can throw the ball.

THE INJURY NOTE YOU SHOULD KNOW

One quick, honest note on the receiver room. Michael Pittman Jr. is on injured reserve with a foot injury. According to the bye week blog, he’s missed the last four games and has played in only three this season. He doesn’t have a firm return date, though he said he’d like to be back after the bye. His own words were measured: “I can’t go out there if I’m not effective.” Labriola added that the bye week doesn’t count toward his required absence.

Meanwhile, Labriola mentioned that Ben Skowronek has been practicing and could be in the mix for the Cincinnati game. Small moves, but they matter for depth.

This is one more reason Warren’s emergence is so important. When the receiver room is thin, the running back has to carry more of the load. And right now, he’s carrying it better than almost anyone in football.

MY TRADE BLOCK BOARD (OPINION, NOT A REPORT)

The deadline is Tuesday, November 10, at 4 p.m. Eastern. Here’s my board, and remember: this is opinion, not a report. I’m not telling you any of these conversations are happening.

Running back: I don’t touch it. With Warren playing at an elite level and Rico Dowdle productive since returning from his injury, this is the deepest position on the offense. There’s no reason to spend a pick here.

Defensive line: I’d look for a cheap veteran rental, but only at a late Day 3 price. Rubio and Jobity have earned snaps, and I don’t want to bury them. A veteran who can rotate for fifteen or twenty plays a game, without blocking the young guys, is the ideal fit.

Receiver: this is still the biggest need. With Pittman on IR and the passing game working out its timing, a one-year veteran who can separate quickly makes sense. Late Day 3 only. And remember, Pittsburgh doesn’t have its own 2027 fourth-round pick, so if the asking price is a fourth, the front office would have to get creative with future selections.

Offensive line: no changes. Six games in the same configuration, third in yards per carry. You don’t mess with chemistry like that in November.

Core players: off the table. Highsmith, Watt, Herbig, Queen, Frazier, Warren. You don’t sell pieces of a first-place team.

FINAL WORD

Here’s my bottom line. The Steelers didn’t go out and find a new star this week. They realized they already had one. Jaylen Warren is second in the NFL in scrimmage yards, seventh in rushing, leads all backs in yards per touch, and has his coach, his quarterback’s top receiver and his center all lining up to praise him. Behind him, Zach Frazier is running the offensive line like a veteran. And on the other side of the ball, two young defensive linemen are turning an injury crisis into an opportunity.

Six and two. First place. A bye week to get healthy and sharpen the passing game. And a rematch in Cincinnati on November 15 that could tighten Pittsburgh’s grip on the division.
